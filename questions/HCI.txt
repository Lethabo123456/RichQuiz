1.9.3 Design Principles Design principles are guidelines that help interaction designers focus on various aspects of the user experience. These principles serve as general abstractions that guide designers in creating more intuitive and effective interfaces. Examples of such principles include: • Feedback: It ensures users know the outcome of their actions, helping them understand what to do next in an interface. For example, when using a device, feedback may come in the form of visual, auditory, or tactile responses. • Findability and Navigability: These principles ensure users can easily locate objects or functions, like navigating a website or finding an option on a device. Clear and structured menus and navigation paths are key here. • Constraints: These are restrictions placed on user interactions to prevent mistakes. For example, certain menu options may be disabled at specific stages of a task. • Consistency: This principle focuses on using uniform operations and elements across the interface to simplify the learning process. Consistent design allows users to predict and understand interactions more easily. • Affordance: Affordance refers to how an object or interface element indicates how it should be used. For example, a button should look like it can be pressed. There are two types of affordance: real affordances (physical objects like a door handle) and perceived affordances (interface elements like buttons or links, which users learn to interact with through experience). These principles are derived from a combination of theory, experience, and common sense and act as triggers for designers to consider various aspects of user interaction. While they don't provide exact solutions, they guide designers in ensuring usability and satisfaction in their designs.

2.2 What Is Involved in Interaction Design? Design exists across various fields, such as graphic design, architectural design, industrial design, and software design, with each having its own unique approach. However, there are common principles shared across these disciplines. The Design Council in the United Kingdom captures these similarities through the "double diamond" design process, as shown in Figure 2.1. This approach is divided into four phases, which are split into two parts. The left side of each diamond represents divergent thinking (broadly exploring the problem), while the right side focuses on convergent thinking (narrowing down to a specific solution). https://www.interaction-design.org/literature/topics/interaction-design-process The four phases are: 1. Discover: Designers start by understanding the problem without making assumptions, engaging with people affected by the issue. 2. Define: Building on insights gained from the Discover phase, designers redefine the challenge in a clearer way. 3. Develop: A variety of solutions are generated, inspired by competing products, insights from earlier phases, and brainstorming. These solutions are co-designed with a diverse group of people. 4. Deliver: The proposed solutions are tested on a small scale, refined, or rejected as necessary to evolve into better solutions. As shown in the diagram, this process is not strictly linear; the phases may be revisited and iterated multiple times as designers move from identifying the challenge to reaching the final outcome. As the Design Council notes, "in a constantly changing and digital world, no idea is ever 'finished.'" The double diamond process is supported by design principles, a set of methods, and two key cultural aspects within organizations: engagement and leadership. The four key design principles that underpin the process focus on people, communication, collaboration, and iteration. These principles align closely with the core values of interaction design. Many of the techniques in the methods supporting the double diamond are also covered in this book. A people-centered design approach emphasizes the importance of involving different stakeholders and fostering conditions conducive to innovation. Importantly, the double diamond approach is relevant not only for new products but also for evolving existing ones and addressing new challenges. 2.2.1 Who to Involve in the Design Process There is a surprisingly broad range of people who have a vested interest in the development of a successful product, known as stakeholders. Stakeholders are individuals or groups who can either influence or be impacted by the success or failure of a project, organization, or product. Stakeholder analysis has been a topic of study for many years (Freeman, 1984), and in recent times, there has been growing attention on involving stakeholders in interaction design. For instance, Maarten Houben and colleagues (2020) conducted workshops with stakeholders to explore requirements for an interactive sound cushion intended for use in a dementia care facility. While some of the stakeholders were potential users of the cushion, others were not. Their stakeholders included roles like care managers, professional caregivers, policymakers, and activity supervisors. https://www.interaction-design.org/literature/article/what-is-interaction-design The group of stakeholders for a specific product is usually broader than the group of actual users. It encompasses customers who purchase the product, users who directly interact with it, developers who design, build, and maintain it, executives who oversee its profitability, legislators who enforce rules on its development and operation, and even people whose lives might be impacted by its launch. The range of stakeholders can be quite extensive. A useful way to identify stakeholders is to ask who has an interest in the project, who has influence or control over it, and who may be affected by its introduction.

2.2.2 The Importance of Involving Users Chapter 1, "What Is Interaction Design?" emphasized the importance of understanding people and their activities when designing interactive products. This involves not only considering users or potential users to understand their needs and activities but also testing and evaluating design concepts within their actual contexts. Both of these aspects are essential to ensure that the final product is usable and provides a positive experience, increasing the likelihood that it will be used. In commercial projects, the role of a product owner is common. The product owner’s responsibility is to filter user and customer feedback into the development process and prioritize features or requirements. Typically, this person has both business and technical knowledge and is frequently involved in assessing designs. While they are closely connected with the product and customer needs, they can only offer a limited perspective on how the product will be used and cannot predict how customers will respond to a particular design in detail. Expectation management refers to ensuring that expectations for the new product are realistic, preventing surprises upon its release. If people feel the product has been misrepresented, it can lead to resistance or rejection. Managing expectations can be especially difficult with large, complex systems. Involving potential users and those affected by the product at various stages of development helps with expectation management by allowing them to understand the product’s capabilities early on. They will gain a clearer understanding of how the product will support their activities, affect their work and lives, and why certain design decisions were made. 2.2.3 Degrees of User Involvement User involvement in the design process can vary greatly, from full engagement throughout every stage of development to more targeted participation in specific activities. It can also range from small groups of users in face-to-face settings to large numbers of users contributing online. Occasionally, users may be integrated into the design team, becoming major contributors to the product’s development. However, this full-time involvement can make them less connected to their original community and context, while part-time involvement may create a heavy workload. On the positive side, having users actively involved in the team, whether full-time or part-time, ensures continuous input. More commonly, users participate in specific activities that help inform development or evaluate designs once available. When user involvement is limited, methods like personas can be used to keep users' concerns at the forefront of the design and development process. User participation can take place in small face-to-face sessions for information gathering or design evaluation, but increasingly, online sessions allow many thousands of users to contribute. While face-to- face involvement and in-situ studies still have value, the range of possibilities for user engagement has expanded. One example of this is online feedback exchange (OFE) systems, which allow millions of potential users to test design concepts before they are released to the market.

2.2.4 What Is a People-Centered Approach? Several decades ago, when the field of human-computer interaction (HCI) was emerging, John Gould and Clayton Lewis (1985) established three key principles they believed would lead to the development of a "useful and easy-to-use computer system." These principles are: • Early focus on users and tasks: This principle emphasizes the importance of first understanding the users by studying their cognitive, behavioral, physical, and attitudinal traits. This involves observing users as they perform their everyday tasks, analyzing those tasks, and involving users in the design process from the outset. • Empirical measurement: Early in the development process, it is crucial to observe and measure the reactions and performance of target users when they interact with materials such as printed scenarios and manuals. As development progresses, users interact with simulations and prototypes, and their reactions and performance are continuously observed, recorded, and analyzed. • Iterative design: This principle involves addressing problems found during user testing and then conducting further tests to evaluate the impact of changes. It emphasizes an iterative cycle of design, testing, measuring, and redesigning, which is repeated until an effective solution is achieved. These principles are the foundation of a user-centered design approach. When they were first introduced, however, they were not widely adopted or understood.

2.2.6 A Simple Lifecycle Model for Interaction Design Understanding the activities involved in interaction design is crucial, but it's equally important to grasp how these activities are interconnected. The term "lifecycle model" (or process model) refers to a framework that outlines a set of activities and their relationships. These models can vary in complexity and are often non-prescriptive. For smaller projects with only a few experienced developers, a straightforward process may be sufficient. However, for larger systems with many developers, a simple approach is inadequate to provide the necessary structure and discipline for developing a usable product. Various lifecycle models have been proposed in fields related to interaction design. For instance, in software engineering, popular models include the waterfall, spiral, and V models (for more details, see Pressman and Maxim [2019]). While HCI has been less focused on lifecycle models, two well-known examples are the Star model (Hartson and Hix, 1989) and the international standard ISO 9241-210. Instead of delving into the specifics of these models, we focus on a simpler lifecycle model depicted in Figure 2.4. This model illustrates how the four activities of interaction design are interconnected and incorporates the principles of people-centered design discussed earlier. Many projects begin by identifying requirements, which then lead to the creation of alternative designs. Prototypes of these designs are developed and evaluated. Based on feedback from evaluations or during the prototyping phase, the team may refine the requirements or redesign the product. This iterative cycle of refinement may happen with one or more alternative designs running in parallel. The underlying idea in this process is that the final product evolves over time, starting from an initial concept to the finished product or gradually progressing from basic functionality to a more advanced one. The exact progression varies from project to project, but development typically concludes with an evaluation activity to ensure the final product meets the expected user experience and usability standards. This evolutionary process is reflected on the right side of the double diamond model, though it’s important to note that discovering requirements may be revisited during this stage. In recent years, numerous lifecycle models have emerged, each encompassing these activities but with different focuses on specific activities, relationships, and outcomes. For example, design sprints (Box 2.3) prioritize rapid investigation of problems, development of solutions, and testing.

2.3 Some Practical Issues The discussion so far has highlighted some issues about the practical application of people centered design and the simple lifecycle of interaction design introduced earlier. These issues are listed here: • How to find out what people need • How to decide what to design • How to generate alternative designs • How to choose among alternatives • How to integrate interaction design activities with other lifecycle models 2.3.1 How to Find Out What People Need If you had asked someone on the street in the late 1990s what they needed, they likely wouldn't have mentioned a smart TV, a ski jacket with an integrated smartphone, or a robotic pet. However, if you had shown them these possibilities and asked if they would buy them, they might have responded more positively. Determining what product to create isn't simply about asking people "What do you need?" and providing it, as people often don’t know what is possible. Suzanne and James Robertson (2013) refer to these as "un-dreamed-of" needs—needs people are unaware they have. Instead of directly asking people, the approach should involve exploring the problem space, understanding potential users, their context, and activities, and identifying areas for improvement. Additionally, experimenting with ideas can help uncover what would truly make a difference. In practice, a combination of these approaches is often employed: trying out ideas to discover requirements and determine what to build, based on a deep understanding of the problem space, potential users, stakeholders, and their activities. Studies in real-world settings or rapid design sprints, which provide real user feedback on early concepts, are particularly valuable for new inventions. Instead of speculating on who might use a product and what they might do with it, it's more effective to introduce the product and observe—the findings can often be unexpected. Many designers and developers have remarked that it’s an “eye-opening experience” when they see users struggle with tasks that seemed clear to them. Focusing on people’s goals, usability goals, and user experience goals offers a more effective approach to interaction design than simply expecting stakeholders to articulate product requirements.

2.3.2 How to Decide What to Design Deciding what to design is a crucial step in the interaction design process. One way to determine this is by exploring the problem space, a step often overlooked by newcomers to the field. When creating or modifying an interactive product, it's tempting to dive directly into the design of the physical interface and the technologies used. The issue with starting here is that it can lead to a misunderstanding of potential users and their context, overlook problems with the existing product, and ignore usability and user experience goals, as discussed in Chapter 1. For example, consider the augmented reality displays and holographic navigation systems in modern cars (see Figure 2.7). These systems are the result of extensive research into human factors, the driving experience, and the suitability of different technologies, as well as technological advancements. Understanding the problem space has been vital in developing solutions that are safe and reliable. While choosing the right technology and designing the physical interface are essential steps, it's best to make these decisions after thoroughly understanding the problem space. This means knowing what the current user experience is, why a change is necessary, and how the proposed changes will improve the experience. In the case of navigation systems, for instance, it's important to identify what issues exist with current systems, such as the distraction caused by small GPS displays while driving. Even when designing for a completely new interactive experience, understanding the context in which the product will be used is crucial. The process of defining the problem space usually involves the entire design team, with members offering different perspectives. For instance, a project manager will likely focus on budgets, timelines, and staffing, while a software engineer will consider the technical aspects of the design. It’s important to consider the implications of each perspective in relation to the others. Although this process can be time-consuming and sometimes lead to disagreements, its benefits are significant: it reduces the likelihood of incorrect assumptions and unsupported claims making their way into the design, which could result in a product that is unusable or unwanted. Taking time to reflect on and discuss ideas during the early stages of design opens up more possibilities and options. Additionally, designers are increasingly expected to justify their choices and present their reasoning in both business and design terms. The ability to think critically, analyze, and present a convincing argument is as highly valued as the ability to create a product (Kolko, 2011).

2.3.3 How to Generate Alternative Designs A common human tendency is to stick with what works, even when a better solution might exist. While it's easy to settle for a solution that feels "good enough," doing so can prevent better alternatives from being considered, which is an important part of the design process. But where do these alternative ideas come from? One source is the designer's own creativity and flair (the "genius design" described in Box 2.1). While it's true that some individuals have a natural talent for coming up with inspired designs, very few ideas are entirely new. Referring to sources of inspiration is a well-known technique for generating ideas in design (Eckert and Stacey, 2000). Research by Pao Siangliulue et al. (2015) shows that people exposed to creative examples tend to generate more innovative ideas than those presented with random ones. Innovations often arise through the cross-fertilization of ideas from various perspectives, people, and contexts, the evolution of existing products based on use and observation, or simply copying ideas from similar products. Cross-fertilization occurs when ideas are presented and discussed within multidisciplinary teams, among other designers, or in workshops with a wide range of stakeholders. For instance, consider the evolution of the cell phone into the smartphone. Initially, the cell phone's purpose was limited to making calls and sending texts, but with time and input from various sources, the smartphone has grown to include features such as photography, streaming media, mobile payments, fitness tracking, and much more. Creativity and invention are often surrounded by mystery, but research has shed light on the process and ways to foster creativity (e.g., Rogers, 2014). For instance, browsing a collection of designs can inspire designers to consider alternative perspectives and, in turn, alternative solutions. As Roger Schank (1982) pointed out, “An expert is someone who gets reminded of just the right prior experience to help him in processing his current experiences.” These experiences don't have to be the designer’s own—they can come from others as well.

2.3.4 How to Choose Among Alternative Designs Choosing between design alternatives often involves making key decisions that affect the final product. These decisions might include aspects such as whether to use a physical keyboard or a touchscreen, or whether an app should automatically save user data. Such choices are influenced by user research, the technical feasibility of ideas, and regulatory considerations, such as security and privacy requirements. Broadly, these decisions can be categorized into two types: those related to visible, measurable features and those concerning internal system characteristics that can't be observed without a deeper analysis. For example, in a printer, visible features include its size, speed, and the paper sizes it supports, while internal factors like material choices (e.g., friction ratings) affect the product’s performance but are not directly observable. In interaction design, the focus is typically on the user experience, making externally visible features the primary concern. Detailed internal workings are relevant primarily when they impact external behaviour or features. To decide between alternative designs, it is essential to let stakeholders interact with them and share their feedback. These designs should be presented in a way that users can easily understand—avoiding technical jargon or overly complex descriptions. Prototypes are often used in this process because they allow users to experience the design first hand. Prototypes help clarify misunderstandings and test technical feasibility, offering better insights into user experience compared to static descriptions. Another approach to comparing designs is through A/B testing, where two variations of a product are deployed, and real user data is gathered to inform the final decision. This method is commonly used for websites, where alternate designs are tested to determine which performs better. Quality is another criterion for choosing between alternatives, but quality is subjective and varies from one stakeholder to another. For instance, one user might value ease of use in a smartphone, while another might prioritize advanced sound settings. To navigate these differences, it’s crucial to understand the varying definitions of quality held by different groups of stakeholders. In many projects, different stakeholder groups define quality in their own ways. For instance, the term “realistic” in a video game might mean different things to developers, teenage players, and parents. Capturing these varying views ensures that expectations are clearly defined and provides a benchmark for evaluating the product.

3.2 Conceptualizing Interaction When starting a design project, it's crucial to clearly define the underlying assumptions and claims. An assumption refers to something that is taken for granted but requires further exploration—such as the belief that people desire fully automated cars. A claim, on the other hand, involves stating something as true, though it remains open to question—like the idea that people will feel safe and enjoy being driven by a car instead of driving it themselves. The key difference is that assumptions relate to current issues or conditions, while claims are projections about future outcomes resulting from a particular action or product introduction. https://www.inf.ed.ac.uk/teaching/courses/hci/1617/lects/Lecture10_conceptualizing.pdf Documenting your assumptions and claims, and then attempting to defend and support them, can help reveal those that are unclear or unsupported. This process allows poorly formulated design ideas to be reworked. In many projects, it involves identifying problematic human activities and determining how they can be improved by introducing a different set of functions. In other cases, it may require more speculative thinking, imagining how to create an engaging user experience that does not yet exist. Box 3.1 presents a hypothetical scenario where a team works through their assumptions and claims, illustrating how this approach helps clarify and explore problems. Sharing assumptions and claims about why a particular idea might be good (or not) allows the design team to gain diverse perspectives on the problem space. This process helps uncover conflicting or problematic views. The following framework offers a set of core questions to guide design teams in this exploration: • Are there issues with the current product or user experience? If so, what are they? • Why do you believe these problems exist? • What evidence supports the presence of these issues? • How do you think your proposed design ideas can address these problems? It is essential to clarify one’s assumptions about an identified problem or new opportunity, as well as the claims made about potential solutions, early in the project and throughout the process. Design teams must also determine the best way to conceptualize the design space. This primarily involves expressing the proposed solution as a conceptual model in terms of the user experience.

3.3 Conceptual Models A model is a simplified representation of a system or process that helps explain how it functions. In this section, we focus on a specific type of model used in interaction design to clarify the problem and design space: the conceptual model. Later, we explore other models that have been developed to explain phenomena in human-computer interaction. Jeff Johnson and Austin Henderson (2002) first defined a conceptual model as "a high-level description of how a system is organized and operates" (p. 26). A key advantage of conceptualizing a design at this level is that it allows designers to refine their thinking before focusing on specific elements of the design (p. 28). In their subsequent handbook on conceptual models, they expanded on this methodology, outlining the steps for creating a conceptual model and how to use it throughout the design process (Johnson and Henderson, 2012). This involves identifying what users can do with a product and the concepts required to understand how to interact with it. Essentially, a conceptual model provides a framework of general concepts and their interconnections, guiding the design process. The core components of a conceptual model include: • Metaphors and analogies that help users understand the product's purpose and how to use it for tasks (e.g., browsing, bookmarking). • Concepts exposed through the product, such as task-domain objects users create or manipulate, their attributes, and the actions that can be performed on them (e.g., saving, revisiting, organizing). • Relationships between these concepts (e.g., a folder containing files). • Mappings between concepts and user experience, showing how the product supports or invokes particular user experiences (e.g., revisiting a page via a list of visited or saved websites). How these metaphors, concepts, and relationships are organized directly impacts the user experience. By discussing these elements, the design team can evaluate different methods of interaction and how they support key concepts like saving, revisiting, categorizing, and reorganizing web pages. They can also explore whether a new overarching metaphor that combines browsing, searching, and revisiting might be more effective. This process can lead the team to identify relationships between the concepts, such as containership, and work out the most intuitive way to organize and revisit saved pages.

Another notable example is the "search engine," a term coined in the early 1990s to describe a software tool that indexes and retrieves files from the Internet using algorithms to match user-selected terms. 3.5 Interaction Types The design space can be conceptualized through the various interaction types that define the user experience. Initially, there were four primary interaction types: instructing, conversing, manipulating, and exploring. A fifth type, responding, was added by Christopher Lueg and others, referring to situations where a system initiates a request that the user can respond to, such as Netflix asking if the user wants to continue watching. Here’s a breakdown of each interaction type: • Instructing: The user issues commands to the system, such as typing in commands, selecting options, speaking commands, gesturing, or pressing buttons. • Conversing: This involves dialogue with a system, often seen in conversational user interfaces (CUIs) where users ask questions and the system responds via text or speech. • Manipulating: Users interact with virtual or physical objects by performing actions like opening, closing, or moving items, relying on their understanding of how objects behave. • Exploring: Users navigate virtual or physical spaces. This includes exploring 3D virtual environments or moving through physical spaces with sensor-based technologies, like smart rooms. • Responding: In this case, the system initiates an interaction, and the user decides whether to engage, such as receiving alerts from a location-based service. These five interaction types are not mutually exclusive, and users may engage with systems in multiple ways, depending on the context. They help guide the design of interfaces and provide insight into how systems should be built to best support users' needs.

3.5.3 Manipulating Manipulating interaction involves people interacting with digital objects by performing actions that reflect how they interact with physical objects in the real world. Common actions include moving, selecting, opening, and closing objects, as well as more advanced actions like zooming in and out or stretching and shrinking—actions not possible with physical objects. Some examples of how this form of interaction is used include: • VR controllers that allow users to manipulate digital objects via physical remotes or gestures. • Gesture control technology in cars, enabling users to interact without physical touch. • Tagged physical objects, such as balls or bricks, that trigger digital events when manipulated (e.g., causing animations or sounds when moved). A key framework that has influenced GUI design is direct manipulation, proposed by Shneiderman in 1983. It suggests that digital objects should be represented in a way that users can manipulate them similarly to physical objects. The core principles of direct manipulation are: 1. Continuous representation of objects and actions of interest. 2. Rapid, reversible, incremental actions with immediate feedback. 3. Physical actions (or button presses) instead of complex commands. These principles enable users to directly control digital objects in an intuitive way. For instance, dragging a file icon into a folder makes the action immediately visible, reinforcing the user's sense of control and progress. The benefits of direct manipulation include: • Quick learning for beginners. • Efficiency for experienced users. • Easy recall of functions for infrequent users. • Immediate visual feedback, which reduces anxiety and prevents errors. • Increased confidence and mastery for users. Apps like word processors, video games, learning tools, and graphical editing software all benefit from these principles. Scrolling with a finger on a touchscreen or using a mouse on a laptop is also a form of direct manipulation, offering a hands-on, intuitive user experience.

4.3.2 Gulfs of Execution and Evaluation The concept of the "gulf of execution" and the "gulf of evaluation" was an early influential framework in Human-Computer Interaction (HCI). It outlined the gaps that exist between the user and the interface, focusing on how designs can bridge these gaps to reduce cognitive effort. The gulf of execution represents the distance from the user to the physical system, while the gulf of evaluation refers to the distance from the system back to the user. Designers are challenged to bridge these gaps to make tasks easier for users. This concept helped in exploring the potential mismatches between a system's design and how users understand and interact with it. 4.3.3 Information Processing One approach to understanding cognitive processes involves using metaphors and analogies. Various comparisons have been made, such as conceptualizing the mind as a reservoir, a telephone network, a digital computer, or a deep learning network. A popular metaphor in the 1980s was the idea of the mind as an information processor. In this view, information enters and exits the mind through a series of ordered processing stages, with different processes, like comparison and response selection, acting upon mental representations. These representations are thought to include images, mental models, rules, and other forms of knowledge. The information processing model helped make predictions about human performance, including reaction times and potential bottlenecks caused by information overload. One early model based on this theory was the human processor model, which described the cognitive processes involved when a user interacts with a computer (Card et al., 1983). It viewed cognition as a series of stages, where perceptual, cognitive, and motor processes were interconnected, predicting the time taken for users to perform various tasks. Although this model was useful in the 1980s for comparing word processors, it has since been largely replaced by more modern models that focus on understanding cognition within its real-world context. Today, the focus is on how external structures, such as the environment, can assist cognition and reduce cognitive load, as seen in models of distributed cognition, external cognition, and embodied cognition.

7.2.1 Command-Line Interfaces Early interactive interfaces primarily relied on command-based input, where users had to type commands or use specific key combinations to interact with the system. Here's an overview of how these interfaces operated and their characteristics: Key Features of Early Command-Based Interfaces: 1. Text Commands at Prompt: o Users typed textual commands, often abbreviations (e.g., ls for listing directory contents in Unix-based systems). o Commands had to follow a strict syntax and format. 2. Key Combinations: o Certain actions required pressing combinations of keys (e.g., Ctrl + V for pasting in many systems). o These combinations provided shortcuts to streamline repetitive tasks. 3. Dedicated Function Keys: o Some keys had predefined actions (e.g., Enter to execute commands, delete to remove content, Escape to cancel operations). o Programmable Function Keys: Users could assign specific commands to function keys (e.g., F11 for a print action). 4. Feedback Loop: o The system responded to commands with text-based output, indicating success, failure, or results (e.g., error messages or lists of files). Advantages: • Efficiency: For experienced users, command-based interfaces allowed rapid execution of tasks. • Low System Resource Usage: Text-based interactions required minimal processing power compared to graphical interfaces. • Flexibility: Users could string together multiple commands to create scripts and automate tasks. Challenges: • Steep Learning Curve: Users needed to memorize commands and their syntax, making the system less accessible for novices. • Error-Prone: Typographical errors in commands often resulted in failure or unintended actions. • Lack of Intuitiveness: The absence of visual guidance made it difficult for new users to learn or explore system functionality. Legacy and Evolution: Command-based interfaces laid the foundation for modern systems. While they have been largely replaced by graphical user interfaces (GUIs) for general users, they remain integral in specific do mains, such as software development, system administration, and data science, where tools like command-line interfaces (CLIs) and terminal applications are still widely used.

8.2.2 Identifying Participants The goals established for a data-gathering session help define the types of individuals from whom data will be collected, referred to as the population or study population. In some instances, this group may be small and easy to identify, such as when there are few stakeholders with accessible contact. However, it is often necessary to select participants from a larger pool, a process known as sampling. When the entire population is accessible, it is called saturation sampling, though this is rare. Typically, only a subset of the population participates, using either probability or nonprobability sampling methods. Probability sampling methods include simple random sampling, where participants are chosen using a random number generator or by selecting every nth person on a list, and stratified sampling, which involves dividing the population into groups (e.g., school classes) before applying random sampling. Nonprobability sampling methods, such as convenience sampling and volunteer panels, rely on participants' availability or willingness to participate. In convenience sampling, the sample comprises those who are readily available rather than specifically selected. A variation of this is snowball sampling, where current participants recruit additional participants, creating a chain effect. The key distinction between probability and nonprobability sampling lies in generalizability. Probability sampling allows for statistical analysis and generalization to the broader population, while nonprobability sampling does not offer robust generalizations. Statistical methods also require a sufficient number of participants. Vera Toepoel (2016) provides an in-depth discussion of sampling, particularly for survey research. Crowdsourcing platforms, such as Prolific, provide access to a large and potentially diverse participant pool, effectively supporting a range of studies. These platforms allow for detailed screening based on various characteristics, including demographics, shopping habits, work situations, hobbies, political or religious beliefs, and even handedness. Specifying participant profiles remains crucial to ensure relevant and meaningful data collection. Further discussions on the use of crowdsourcing and evaluation can be found in Chapters 10 and 14.

8.2.3 Relationship with Participants A crucial element of data gathering is the relationship between the researchers and the participants. 8.2.5 Triangulation Triangulation refers to studying a phenomenon from at least two different perspectives (Denzin, 2006; Jupp, 2006), and four types have been defined by Jupp (2006): 1. Data Triangulation involves gathering data from various sources, times, locations, or individuals, potentially using different sampling techniques. 2. Investigator Triangulation refers to involving multiple researchers in the collection and interpretation of data, such as different observers or interviewers. 3. Theory Triangulation uses multiple theoretical frameworks to interpret the data or findings. 4. Methodological Triangulation employs different data collection techniques. The most common form of triangulation is methodological triangulation, which validates findings by pointing to similar results achieved through different perspectives. However, achieving true triangulation is challenging because various data collection methods generate different types of data that may not always be compatible. Similarly, using different theoretical frameworks may not necessarily yield complementary results, and for theoretical triangulation to be effective, the frameworks must share similar philosophical foundations. While true triangulation is difficult, combining multiple data collection techniques and analysis approaches often provides valuable insights. Another type of triangulation focuses on data verification and reliability, known as "ground truth" validation. This is commonly applied in studies involving large datasets, such as crowdsourcing and machine learning, to ensure the authenticity and reliability of the data. However, identifying ground truth can be complex. In some fields, self-reported or human-labeled data is considered ground truth, but the accuracy of such labeling can be questionable. 8.4 Interviews Interviews are often described as a "conversation with a purpose" (Kahn and Cannell, 1957). The level of similarity to a typical conversation depends on the interview type, which falls into four categories: open- ended (unstructured), structured, semi-structured, and group interviews. The first three types are distinguished by the degree of control the interviewer has, based on whether they follow a predetermined set of questions. The fourth type, known as a focus group, involves a small group guided by a facilitator, which may range from informal to structured facilitation. The choice of interview approach depends on the interview's purpose, the questions being addressed, and the interaction design activity. For example, if the goal is to explore people’s impressions of a new design concept, an informal, open-ended interview is often most effective. However, if the aim is to gather feedback on a specific design feature, such as the layout of a web browser, a structured interview or questionnaire is typically preferred due to its more focused questions. The questions posed are open-ended, meaning there is no specific expectation for the format or content of the answers. For example, an initial question could be: “What are the advantages and disadvantages of using a wearable?” The interviewee has the freedom to answer in any way they choose, and both the interviewer and the interviewee can guide the conversation. The interviewer might ask follow-up questions like, “Can you tell me more about that?” which is known as probing. Although unstructured interviews are open-ended, the interviewer should have a plan outlining the key topics to ensure that all relevant areas are covered. It’s important to differentiate between being open to new ideas and having no plan at all. 8.4.3 Semi-Structured Interviews Semi-structured interviews blend elements of both structured and unstructured interviews, incorporating both closed and open questions. The interviewer follows a basic script to ensure all topics are covered, starting with pre-planned questions and then probing the interviewee for more information until no new relevant insights emerge. For example, if an interviewee mentions a specific music website they prefer, the interviewer might probe further by asking why they like it and for more details about the site’s layout. It’s important for the interviewer not to lead the interviewee with biased questions that imply a certain answer, such as suggesting the interviewee liked a particular aspect of the site. The interviewer’s body language can also influence responses, so it’s crucial to remain neutral and give the interviewee time to answer. Probes, like “Do you want to tell me anything else?” or prompts that remind the interviewee of forgotten terms, help to keep the interview on track without introducing bias. While semi-structured interviews are flexible and can be tailored based on the conversation, they are designed to be broadly replicable, with probing and prompting used to gather additional information without leading the interviewee. 8.4.4 Focus Groups Interviews are typically one-on-one, but group interviews, such as focus groups, are also common in interaction design activities. Focus groups usually involve three to ten participants, guided by a trained facilitator, and are designed to represent a cross-section of the target population. For example, in evaluating an interactive university campus map, separate focus groups may be formed for administrators, faculty, students, and visitors, as each group uses the map differently. 9.3 Design Patterns Design patterns encapsulate design knowledge, offering a unique structure and philosophy compared to other methods or forms of guidance. A primary goal of the patterns community is to establish a shared vocabulary—anchored by the names of the patterns—that enables designers to communicate effectively with one another and with stakeholders. Another aim is to document design expertise in a compelling and accessible way. The concept of patterns originated from architect Christopher Alexander, who introduced patterns in architecture (Alexander, 1979). His vision was to capture the "quality without a name"—a sense of inherent goodness that is instantly recognizable. In design, a pattern is essentially a solution to a recurring problem within a specific context. Each pattern describes the problem, the solution, and the circumstances in which the solution has been successful, along with the rationale for its effectiveness. A defining feature of design patterns is their generative nature—they can be implemented in various ways, making them highly adaptable. Designers favor patterns because they offer proven solutions to familiar challenges. Many pattern repositories also provide accompanying code snippets, often hosted on open-source platforms like GitHub. These snippets can frequently be integrated with minimal modification, saving time and effort. Because patterns represent familiar solutions, they enhance the user experience of new products by leveraging designs that users already recognize and understand. Design patterns are a distilled representation of common practices, but one of the challenges with common practices is that they are not always effective. Design approaches that embody poor practices are known as anti-patterns. A classic example of an anti-pattern is "click here," also referred to as mystery navigation. This is problematic because it fails to indicate to the user what action will result from clicking the link, leading to poor interaction design. Despite significant improvements in interaction design and user experience since the first edition of this book in 2002, anti-patterns persist. This is partly because technology evolves rapidly, and design solutions that work well on one platform may not be as effective on others. Furthermore, as patterns are used more widely, a better understanding of their advantages and drawbacks emerges, and sometimes patterns are applied in unintended ways. A prime example is the hamburger menu, originally designed for contextual menus but repurposed to save screen space. Another important concept in design patterns is dark patterns. While not inherently poor design, dark patterns are intentionally crafted to deceive users, prioritizing organizational benefits over user value. Some dark patterns are accidental, and once identified, they can be quickly corrected. However, when a UX designer intentionally leverages knowledge of human behavior to implement misleading functionality that harms the user experience, that is considered a dark pattern. Research by Linda Di Geronimo et al. (2020), which analyzed 240 mobile apps with 589 users, found that popular apps often employ at least seven types of deceptive interfaces. 9.4 Open Source Resources Open source software refers to source code for components, frameworks, or entire systems that are freely available for reuse or modification. Design systems are often released in open source repositories for others to access and utilize, such as Microsoft's Fluent Design System. Open source development is a community-driven effort, where developers contribute, maintain, and enhance code, which is then shared with the community through open source repositories for further development and use. The contributors, or committers, are mostly volunteers, although an increasing number of companies are also releasing open source code. These components are made available for use under licenses that allow anyone to use, modify, and adapt the software without the typical copyright restrictions.
