• Measures of Centre: 
o Primarily the Mode: The most common category in a dataset is considered the 
centre. 
o Example: In a survey, the most common continent being Africa would make it 
the mode. 
o Note: Mean and median are not applicable as nominal data cannot be ordered 
or summed. 
 
• Considerations: 
o Categorical Nature: Nominal data is categorical and subject to translation or 
spelling errors. 
o Limitation in Insights: The data is limited in providing insights, as we cannot 
define an average or typical case. 
 
The Ordinal Level of Data 
Definition and Characteristics: 
• Enhanced Flexibility: Ordinal data introduces the concept of order or ranking, allowing 
us to arrange data sequentially. 
• Lack of Quantitative Differences: While we can rank items, the actual numerical 
difference between them is not meaningful. 
Examples and Explanation: 
Likert Scale: 
• Description: Ratings (e.g., 1 to 10) for satisfaction or agreement are ordinal. 
• Note: While 8 is better than 7, the exact difference between these ratings is not 
quantitatively defined. 
Visible Light Spectrum: 
• Ordering: colours like red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet can be ordered 
in terms of energy. 
• Insight: This order represents a natural progression but does not quantify the 
difference in energy levels. 
Mathematical Operations: 
• Inherited from Nominal Level: Equality and set membership. 
• Additional Operations: 
o Ordering: Establishing a sequence based on the data's natural order. 
o Comparison: Determining if one item ranks higher or lower than another. 
Application in Analysis: 
• Interpretation of Data: Understanding ordinal data involves recognizing the rank 
order but being cautious about interpreting differences as exact measurements. 
• Example in Research: In surveys, the ordinal scale helps in understanding preferences 
or perceptions, but the researcher must be aware of its limitations in quantitative 
analysis.
