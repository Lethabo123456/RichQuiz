Reducing the distance between points has resulted in ever smaller chips, with the circuits 
packed closer together. Gordon Moore, who would cofound Intel (the largest maker of micro- 
processor chips) and become its chairman of the board, hypothesized that progress in chip 
manufacturing ought to make it possible to double the number of transistors (the microscopic 
on off switches) on a single chip every two years. The hypothesis became known as Moore’s 
Law, and this ‘rule of thumb’ has become a goal that chip manufacturers have met more or less

for more than four decades. Chip manufacturers have been able to improve productivity and 
performance by putting more transistors on the same size chip while reducing the amount of 
power required to perform tasks. Furthermore, because the chips are smaller, chip 
manufacturers can cut more chips from a single silicon wafer and thus reduce the cost per chip.
