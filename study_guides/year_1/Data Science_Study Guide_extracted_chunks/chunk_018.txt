• Organized data: This refers to data that is sorted into a row/column structure, where every 
row represents a single observation and the columns represent the characteristics of that 
observation (Sinan, 2016). 
• Unorganized data: This is the type of data that is in the free form, usually text or raw 
audio/signals that must be parsed further to become organized. Whenever you open Excel (or 
any other spreadsheet program), you are looking at a blank row/column structure waiting for 
organized data. These programs don't do well with unorganized data. For the most part, we 
will deal with organized data as it is the easiest to glean insight from, but we will not shy away 
from looking at raw text and methods of processing unorganized forms of data (Sinan, 2016). 
Most scientific fields commonly refer to data as unstructured and raw, which implies it serves very 
little to no meaning. For example, the weight or height of each learner in your classroom. Having a 
collection of this information will serve very little purpose. However, if we process this information 
into a more structured manner are we able to infer some meaning from it. Such as the average weight 
or height and the tallest or shortest learner etc. This meaning is now referred to as information. Which 
the fundamental purpose of a Data Scientist (i.e. to derive meaning from a corpus of data).
