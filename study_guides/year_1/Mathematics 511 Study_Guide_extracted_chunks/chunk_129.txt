2.1.3 Duality 
The identities in the previous example above come in pairs (except for the 
law of the double complement and the unit and zero properties). To explain 
the relationship between the two identities in each pair we use the concept of a dual. 
The dual of a Boolean expression is obtained by interchanging Boolean sums 
and Boolean products and interchanging 0s and 1s. All the definitions states, that if a statement 
is true, then also its dual statement is true and this dual statement is obtained by 
changing + for ∙, 0 for 1 and vice versa. However when we say 1+1, whose dual 
statement according to the above is 0.0, I get opposite results, that is:  
               
1 + 1 = 1, 0 . 0 = 0. How should you understand this duality principle?                  
"1+1=0 " is a statement (a boolean statement), and indeed, 1+1=1  happens 
to be a true statement. Likewise, the entire statement "0⋅0=0 " is a true 
statement, since 0⋅0  correctly evaluates to false: and this is exactly what 
"0⋅0=0 " asserts, so it is a correct (true) statement about the falsity of 0⋅0 The 
duality principle ensures that "if we exchange every symbol by its dual in a 
formula, we get the dual result".
