1.3 Propositional Equivalences 
Introduction 
An important type of step used in a mathematical argument is the 
replacement of a statement with another statement with the same truth 
value. Because of this, methods that produce propositions with the same 
truth value as a given compound proposition are used extensively in the 
construction of mathematical arguments. Note that we will use the term 
“compound proposition” to refer to an expression formed from 
propositional variables using logical operators, such as 𝑝 ∧ 𝑞. 

Definition 8 
A compound proposition that is always true, no matter what the truth values 
of the propositional variables that occur in it, is called a tautology.                         
A compound proposition that is always false is called a contradiction. A 
compound proposition that is neither a tautology nor a contradiction is 
called a contingency. 
We can construct examples of tautologies and contradictions using just one 
propositional variable. Consider the truth tables of 𝑝 ∨￢𝑝 and 𝑝 ∧￢𝑝, 
shown in Table 10. Because 𝑝 ∨￢𝑝 is always true, it is a tautology. Because 
𝑝 ∧￢𝑝 is always false, it is a contradiction.
