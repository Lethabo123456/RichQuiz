Definition 1 
The universal quantification of 𝑃(𝑥) is the statement: 
“𝑃(𝑥) for all values of 𝑥 in the domain.” 

The notation ∀𝑥𝑃(𝑥) denotes the universal quantification of 𝑃(𝑥). Here ∀ is 
called the universal quantifier. We read ∀𝑥𝑃(𝑥) as “for all 𝑥𝑃(𝑥)” or “for 
every 𝑥𝑃(𝑥). ” An element for which 𝑃(𝑥) is false is called a counterexample 
of ∀𝑥𝑃(𝑥).

Remark: Generally, an implicit assumption is made that all domains of 
discourse for quantifiers are nonempty. Note that if the domain is empty, 
then ∀𝑥𝑃(𝑥) is true for any propositional function 𝑃(𝑥) because there are 
no elements x in the domain for which 𝑃(𝑥) is false. 
Besides “for all” and “for every,” universal quantification can be expressed 
in many other ways, including “all of,” “for each,” “given any,” “for 
arbitrary,” “for each,” and “for any.” 

Remark: It is best to avoid using “for any 𝑥” because it is often ambiguous as 
to whether “any” means “every” or “some.” In some cases, “any” is 
unambiguous, such as when it is used in negatives, for example, “there is not 
any reason to avoid studying.”

that 𝑃(𝑥) is not always true when 𝑥 is in the domain is to find a 
counterexample to the statement ∀𝑥𝑃(𝑥). Note that a single
