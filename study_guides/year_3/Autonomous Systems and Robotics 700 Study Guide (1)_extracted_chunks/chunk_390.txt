6.1.1 Supervised Learning in Robotics 
At its core, supervised learning involves: 
• A training set composed of input-output pairs. 
• A learning algorithm that generalizes from these examples. 
• The goal: to find a hypothesis (a function) that accurately predicts outputs for new, 
unseen inputs. 
This process is akin to teaching by example. For instance, we might train a robot to distinguish 
between "obstacle" and "open path" by showing it hundreds of labeled images. 

6.1.1.1 Components of Supervised Learning 
1. Hypothesis Space 
The set of all possible functions the learner could choose from. Choosing a good hypothesis space 
is crucial — too simple, and the model underfits; too complex, and it overfits. 
2. Loss Function (Error Function) 
Measures the difference between predicted output and actual output. Learning aims to minimize 
this loss over the training data. 
3. Inductive Bias 
Since the true function is unknown, the learner must rely on assumptions or "biases" to generalize 
well. For example, a bias might be that simpler hypotheses are more likely to be correct (Occam’s 
Razor).

6.1.1.2 Examples of Supervised Learning Tasks 
• Classification: Assigning discrete labels to inputs. e.g., Classifying emails as spam or not spam. 
• Regression: Predicting continuous values. e.g., Predicting the angle a robotic arm should 
move to.
