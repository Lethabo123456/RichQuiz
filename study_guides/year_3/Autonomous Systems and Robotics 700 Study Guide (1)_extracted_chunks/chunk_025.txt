1.6  Automation Levels and Conceptual Framework 
Manual systems and fully automated systems represent two ends of the spectrum in operational 
and technological processes, each with distinct characteristics, applications, advantages, and 
disadvantages. Manual systems are those where human effort drives all processes, requiring little 
to no reliance on technology. These systems are prevalent in scenarios where advanced 
technological infrastructure is either unavailable or unnecessary, such as traditional farming, 
small-scale craft production, or manual bookkeeping. One of the most significant advantages of 
manual systems is their flexibility; humans can adapt to unforeseen situations and make judgment 
calls that machines cannot. 

This human-centric control fosters creativity and problem-solving, essential in domains 
requiring customisation or non-standardised outputs. Additionally, manual systems avoid the 
high costs associated with deploying and maintaining advanced technological infrastructure, 
making them suitable for low-resource settings. However, manual systems also have notable 
drawbacks. The reliance on human effort can lead to slower processes, especially for repetitive 
or labour-intensive tasks. Furthermore, human operators are prone to fatigue, errors, and 
variability in performance, which can compromise efficiency and consistency. In contexts where 
precision and speed are paramount, such as high-volume manufacturing, the limitations of 
manual systems become particularly evident.
