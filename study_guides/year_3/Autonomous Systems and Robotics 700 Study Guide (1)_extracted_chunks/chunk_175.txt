4.4 Kinematic and Dynamic Models  
4.4.1 Kinematics Models 
Kinematics is the description of the motion of points, bodies, and systems of bodies. It only 
describes how things are moving, but not why. To describe the kinematics of a moving point, we 
will refer to position vectors, which are generically defined in R3, and their derivatives.  
Kinematic Models: These models describe the geometry of robot movement without considering 
the forces and torques that cause the movement. They are essential for answering questions like: 
"If my robot's joints are at these angles, where is its end-effector?". 
Forward Kinematics: Calculates the position and orientation of the end-effector (e.g., a robotic 
arm's gripper) given the joint angles. This is often solved using transformation matrices and 
methods like the Denavit-Hartenberg (DH) convention. 

Inverse Kinematics: Calculates the required joint angles to achieve a desired position and 
orientation of the end-effector. This is often more complex and may have multiple solutions or no 
solution at all.

4.4.2 Dynamic Models  
These models describe the relationship between the forces and torques acting on the robot and 
its resulting motion. They are crucial for designing controllers that can handle inertia, gravity, and 
other external forces. 
Lagrange-Euler Formulation: A common method for deriving the equations of motion for a robot, 
which relates the robot's kinetic and potential energy to the applied forces and torques. 
Newton-Euler Formulation: Another method that applies Newton's second law and the principle 
of conservation of angular momentum to each link of the robot. 
Using a physics engine is essential for simulating dynamic models. Libraries and frameworks like 
PyBullet, Mujoco, and Gazebo provide robust physics simulation, collision detection, and sensor 
modelling, allowing you to test controllers in a realistic virtual environment.
