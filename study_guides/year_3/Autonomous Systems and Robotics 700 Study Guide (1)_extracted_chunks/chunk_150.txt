4.2  Fundamental Architecture 
The control architecture of software-based robots typically follows a hierarchical structure with 
multiple layers of abstraction. At the lowest level, servo controllers manage individual actuators 
using feedback loops to maintain precise positioning and velocity control. These controllers 
implement algorithms such as PID (Proportional-Integral-Derivative) control to minimise the error 
between the desired and actual states. 
Moving up the hierarchy, motion controllers coordinate multiple actuators to achieve complex 
movements. These systems handle trajectory planning, interpolation, and the synchronisation of 
multiple degrees of freedom. They translate high-level motion commands into sequences of low-
level actuator instructions while respecting physical constraints such as acceleration limits and 
collision avoidance. 

4.3  PID Controllers 
A PID controller is a control loop feedback mechanism that calculates the difference between a 
desired value (setpoint) and the actual output from a process and use that result to apply a 
correction to the process. The term PID stands for Proportional–Integral–Derivative feedback 

control, and it is one of the most commonly used controllers in the industry. It is the best starting 
point when designing an autonomous control system and is very popular in commercial autopilot 
systems and open-source developments. The main goal of this process is to maintain a specified 
setpoint value. For example, you may want a DC motor to maintain a setpoint value r(t) of 600 
encoder pulses per second. The actual motor speed y(t), referred to as the process variable, is 
subtracted from the setpoint value of 600 to determine the error value e(t). The PID controller 
then computes the new control value u(t) to apply to the motor based on the computed error 
value. In the case of a DC motor, the control value would be a pulse-width-modulated (PWM) 
signal.
