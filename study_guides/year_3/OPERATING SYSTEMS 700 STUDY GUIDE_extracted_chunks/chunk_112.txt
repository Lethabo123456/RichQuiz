2. Non-Preemptive Priority Scheduling: 
 
Once a process starts execution, it cannot be interrupted until it completes. 
Lower-priority processes must wait until the CPU becomes available. 
Priorities can also be determined by the Processor Manager based on characteristics intrinsic to the 
jobs such as: 

➢ Memory requirements. Jobs requiring large amounts of memory could be allocated lower 
priorities than those requesting small amounts of memory, or vice versa. 
➢ Number and type of peripheral devices. Jobs requiring many peripheral devices could be 
allocated lower priorities than those requesting fewer devices. 
➢ Total CPU time. Jobs having a long CPU cycle, or estimated run time, could begiven lower 
priorities than those having a brief estimated run time. 
➢ Amount of time already spent in the system. This is the total amount of elapsed time since the 
job was accepted for processing. Some systems increase the priority of jobs that have been in 
the system for an unusually long time to expedite their exit. This is known as aging.

3.6.4 Shortest Remaining Time (SRT) 
Shortest Remaining Time (SRT) is a preemptive version of the Shortest Job Next (SJN) scheduling 
algorithm. In SRT, the CPU is allocated to the process that has the smallest remaining burst time. 
This means that if a new process arrives with a burst time smaller than the remaining time of the 
current process, the CPU is preempted and given to the new process. 

3.6.5 Round Robin (RR) Scheduling 
Round Robin (RR) is a preemptive CPU scheduling algorithm designed to handle time-sharing 
systems. Each process is assigned a fixed time quantum or time slice. The CPU cycles through all 
processes in the ready queue, executing each for a maximum of one-time quantum at a time. If a 
process does not complete during its time quantum, it is moved to the end of the queue.
