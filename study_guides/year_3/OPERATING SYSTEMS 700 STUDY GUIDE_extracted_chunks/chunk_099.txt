3.3. Process Scheduler 
Once a job is accepted by the Job Scheduler, the Process Scheduler manages its execution. If the 
operating system supports threads, the Process Scheduler oversees them as well. It determines 
which processes receive CPU time, when, and for how long. Additionally, it handles interruptions, 
assigns jobs to appropriate queues during execution, and terminates jobs once they are completed. 
 
As a low-level scheduler, the Process Scheduler is responsible for allocating the CPU to execute 
tasks for jobs placed in the READY queue by the Job Scheduler. This coordination is critical, 
especially when managing multiple jobs that require efficient orchestration, like prioritizing tasks 
in a real-life scenario. 
 
3.3.1. Job and Process States 
As a job, process, or thread moves through the system, its status changes, often from HOLD, to 
READY, to RUNNING, to WAITING, and, eventually, to FINISHED, as shown in the figure 3.1 
below these are called the job status, process status, or thread status, respectively. 
 
Figure 3.1 
 
When a user submits a job, its status transitions through several states, guided by the Job Scheduler 
and Process Scheduler. 

1. HOLD: Upon submission, the job enters the HOLD state and is queued. A job spooler may record 
its attributes (e.g., CPU time estimate, priority, required I/O devices, and memory needs) in a table, 
which the Job Scheduler uses to decide execution order. 
2. READY: After system resources are checked and interrupts resolved, the job moves to READY. Some 
systems may place jobs directly into the READY queue. 
3. RUNNING: The Process Scheduler assigns the CPU to the job, marking it as RUNNING. In single- 
processor systems, only one job can be in this state at a time. 
4. WAITING: If a job requires a resource or an I/O operation, it moves to WAITING. Once the need is 
fulfilled, the job returns to READY. 
5. FINISHED: After successful completion or due to an error, the job is marked as FINISHED and is 
returned to the user or terminated.
