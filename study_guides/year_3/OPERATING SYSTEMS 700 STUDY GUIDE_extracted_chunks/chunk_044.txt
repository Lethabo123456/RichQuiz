Best-fit allocation offers a balance between efficient memory utilization and complexity in 
management. It is most effective when the free list is well-organized, and the overhead of 
searching for the best fit does not outweigh the benefits of reduced fragmentation. For systems 
with dynamic and unpredictable memory demands, careful consideration is needed to determine 
whether the benefits of this approach justify its computational cost.

First-Fit Allocation 
First-Fit Allocation is a memory management strategy where the operating system allocates the 
first available partition in memory that is large enough to accommodate an incoming job or 
process. This approach is simple and fast because the system does not need to search the entire 
list of free memory spaces, it stops as soon as it finds a suitable partition. While this method 
reduces search time and overhead, it often results in external fragmentation because it does not 
prioritize fitting jobs into partitions with minimal leftover space. 
For both fixed and dynamic memory allocation schemes, the operating system also maintains two 
key lists (the free list and the busy list). The free list tracks all unoccupied memory locations, while 
the busy list records all allocated partitions. When a new job arrives, the operating system scans 
the free list sequentially and assigns the first partition that can fit the job. The corresponding 
partition is then removed from the free list and added to the busy list. If the partition size is larger 
than the job, the leftover space remains in the free list as a smaller partition. 
In practical terms, imagine you are managing a small library with books of various sizes and 
shelves of fixed capacities. Whenever a book is returned, you scan the shelves from the top down 
and place the book on the first shelf that has enough room, regardless of whether a better-fitting 
shelf exists further down. For example, if a small paperback is returned, it might be placed on a 
large shelf simply because it is the first available space. This approach may lead to inefficiencies, 
as smaller books fill up large spaces, leaving fragmented gaps that cannot accommodate other 
books. 

As books are checked out or returned, the system updates the lists. When a book is removed, the 
space is taken off the busy list and added back to the free list. Similarly, when a new book is 
placed on a shelf the system finds the first available spot, updates the lists accordingly, and leaves 
any leftover space as a smaller partition in the free list. 
The free list in First-Fit Allocation is typically organized by location, meaning memory partitions 
are listed in the order in which they appear in physical memory. This makes it easier for the system 
to allocate the first suitable partition. However, the simplicity of this method can lead to external 
fragmentation as memory becomes increasingly divided into small unusable chunks over time. 
To address this, periodic defragmentation may be required to consolidate free spaces into larger 
contiguous blocks.
