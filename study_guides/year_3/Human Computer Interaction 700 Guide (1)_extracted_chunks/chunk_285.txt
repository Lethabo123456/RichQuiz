7.3 Natural User Interfaces and Beyond 
Over the years, various types of interfaces have been developed to enhance user experiences. Initially, 
graphical user interfaces (GUIs) dominated, followed by mobile device interfaces, touch interfaces, and 
more recently, wearables and smart interfaces. Looking ahead, holographic interfaces may become 
prevalent. These advancements have undoubtedly supported diverse user activities, but what lies 
beyond? Could we witness the mainstream adoption of interfaces designed to feel even more natural? 

Natural user interfaces (NUIs) aim to enable users to interact with computers similarly to how they 
interact with the physical world—using voice, hands, and body movements. Unlike traditional input 
methods like keyboards, mice, or touchpads, NUIs allow users to communicate with devices through 
speech, touch, gestures, or facial expressions. These interfaces leverage innate human abilities such as 
talking, writing, gesturing, walking, and manipulating objects, theoretically making them more intuitive 
and easier to learn compared to GUIs.
 
For example, instead of memorizing function keys to open a file, a user can simply raise their arm or say 
“open.” However, the naturalness of NUIs can be subjective. Is it more intuitive to say “open” than to flip 
a switch? Is raising both arms to change a TV channel more natural than pressing a button on a remote or 
giving a voice command? Don Norman (2010) posited in his critique of NUIs that their perceived 
naturalness depends on factors like the learning curve, interface complexity, and the need for accuracy 
and speed. In some cases, gestures may be more effective than words, while in others, the reverse is true, 
depending on the system's functionality. 

Consider sensor-based faucets, which typically support two functions: turning water on by waving a hand 
and turning it off by removing the hand. These simple gestures are intuitive for these tasks. However, 
adding functions like adjusting water temperature or flow complicates the interface. How should gestures 
for such controls be designed? Would raising one arm set the temperature while the other adjusts flow? 
How would the system confirm the settings without confusing subsequent actions? These challenges 
highlight why public sensor-based faucets often default to pre-set temperatures and flows. 
Our exploration of interface types reveals that gestural, voice, and other innovative interfaces have 
enhanced digital interactions, making them more accessible and enjoyable. For instance, gestures and 
full-body movements have been embraced in gaming and fitness, providing engaging experiences. 
Additionally, advanced gesture, voice, and touch interfaces have significantly improved accessibility for 
visually impaired users. Features like the iPhone’s VoiceOver enable visually impaired individuals to 
navigate applications, send emails, and browse the web without requiring specialized devices, fostering 
inclusivity and reducing stigmatization. 
On the other hand, brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) represent a stark contrast. Controlling devices 
through thought alone is an unfamiliar concept for most people, requiring substantial training to achieve 
even basic actions, such as selecting a letter on a screen. 
Nevertheless, technological advancements in brain, body, behavioral, and environmental sensors now 
allow for real-time detection of subtle cognitive and emotional changes. This capability has opened new 
possibilities in human-computer interaction, where continuous and discrete inputs can dynamically adapt 
to user needs. When combined with artificial intelligence, these interfaces transcend being merely natural 
or smart. They pave the way for synergistic collaboration between humans and technology, empowering 
individuals to tackle increasingly complex challenges and accomplish previously unimaginable feats.
