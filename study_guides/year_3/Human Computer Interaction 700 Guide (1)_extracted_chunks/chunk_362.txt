9.2.2 Aligning Work Practices 
 
User research, also known as discovery research, involves collecting and analyzing data to understand 
potential users, their tasks, and the context in which they operate. While this research is typically 
conducted before product development begins, it remains valuable throughout the development process. 
In agile environments, time-intensive methods like ethnography are incompatible with short iteration 
cycles. As a result, technical development should proceed independently of such studies. Instead, agile 
projects benefit from focused activities, such as evaluating design components, clarifying requirements, 
or understanding task contexts, which can run concurrently with development. This is exemplified by the 
dual tracks approach. 
Bob Thomas (2021) recommends adopting lean user research methods, which involve team members 
from technical and design roles participating in usability tests, recording observations on sticky notes, and 
summarizing findings within hours or days rather than weeks. 

One strategy is to conduct user research before the project officially starts, often referred to as "iteration 
zero." This phase supports foundational activities, including software architecture design and user 
research. However, the time allotted for iteration zero may still be insufficient for more extensive 
research. Don Norman (2006) suggests conducting user research even earlier, before a project is 
announced, to avoid the time constraints imposed by agile timeboxes. 
Another approach involves maintaining a continuous user research program that regularly updates and 
enhances a company's understanding of its users over an extended period. This ongoing effort informs 
individual projects, with iteration zero focused on collecting project-specific data. For example, companies 
like Microsoft and Google recruit participants for various research activities, using the insights gained to 
guide product development. 

Cycles 0 and 1 differ significantly from subsequent cycles in agile development, as the initial focus is on 
creating a product vision. While agile methodologies handle this differently, all agree on the importance 
of upfront work to understand the product, its scope, and its overall design—both technical and UX. 
Preliminary customer and behavioral data may be collected before cycle 0, but the vision and overarching 
design for the current project are finalized by the end of cycle 0. The nature of the product—whether it’s 
an update, a new product, or an entirely novel experience—affects the work required. Cycle 0 might be 
longer than other cycles to meet these needs, but it does not aim to deliver pixel-perfect designs before 
the evolutionary development begins. 

Cycle 1 typically involves technical setup activities, allowing UX designers to prepare designs and user 
activities for cycle 2. Subsequent cycles follow a rhythm where design and user activities occur in cycle n–
1, while corresponding technical activities happen in cycle n. 
When this dual-track approach was first introduced, interaction designers recognized three key 
advantages. First, design time was not wasted on features that wouldn’t be implemented. Second, 
combining usability testing and contextual inquiry during the same customer visit saved time. Third, 
designers received timely feedback from users and developers, allowing them to adjust designs based on 
feedback. For example, schedules could be revised for longer development times, or features could be 
dropped if users prioritized other needs. 

The dual-track methodology, widely known as agile UX, has become a common way to implement agile 
practices. UX designers sometimes work two iterations ahead, depending on the work required, iteration 
length, and stakeholder input timelines. Despite the parallel nature of the design and developer tracks, 
they are closely interconnected and should not be treated as separate processes. 
This method aligns with the double diamond process, where the design track focuses on “discovery” 
(understanding pain points), and the developer track emphasizes “delivery” (implementing solutions). 
Discovery activities include stakeholder interviews, user research, creating personas, and story mapping 
to prioritize features. 

Since its introduction in the early 2000s, this approach has evolved, particularly for larger, long-term 
projects with ample resources. In such cases, the design and developer tracks are less tightly coupled, and 
design iterations may vary in length. Some ideas from discovery may remain in refinement for longer or 
may never progress to delivery. However, for smaller teams and short projects, this flexibility could lead 
to role overburdening due to the demands of managing multiple activities.
