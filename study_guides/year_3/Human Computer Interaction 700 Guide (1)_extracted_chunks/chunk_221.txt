5.5 Co-Presence  
 
Co-presence is a concept in human-computer interaction that focuses on designing social technologies to 
enhance people's activities while they interact in the same physical space. The goal is to help co-located 
groups collaborate more efficiently in various settings such as work, learning, and socialization. Several 
technologies have been developed to facilitate this type of parallel interaction, including multitouch, mid-
air gesture recognition, and object recognition systems. To evaluate their effectiveness, it is crucial to first 
understand the coordination and awareness mechanisms people already use in face-to-face interactions 
and how these mechanisms are either adapted or replaced by technological tools. 

5.5.1 Physical Coordination  
When people work closely together, they often communicate both verbally and nonverbally to coordinate 
their actions. For instance, during a collaborative task like moving a piano, people issue commands such 
as "Down a bit," "Left a touch," or "Now go forward" to guide the process. Nonverbal cues such as nods, 
hand gestures, glances, and raised hands complement or even replace verbal instructions to enhance 
communication. 
In time-sensitive and routine collaborative tasks, especially where communication may be difficult due to 
environmental conditions, people often rely on gestures. These gestures, which may include standardized 
hand signals or radio-controlled communication systems, help synchronize actions. For example, a 
conductor’s baton movements guide the orchestra, and airport ground personnel use hand signals to 

direct pilots to their gates. Universal gestures like beckoning, waving, or stopping are commonly used, 
though their meaning can vary by culture. 
Physical objects, such as wands or batons, can also aid coordination. These items serve as external tools 
for explaining ideas or plans and can draw attention effectively when held up. Their use allows group 
members to consider more options and enhances the overall awareness of each other's activities. 

5.5.2 Awareness  
Awareness, a term coined by Dourish and Bly (1992), refers to understanding who is around, what is 
happening, and who is interacting with whom. For example, at a party, people move around, observe 
events, and overhear conversations to stay informed about the social dynamics. A specific type of 
awareness, called peripheral awareness, allows individuals to keep track of what is happening in their 
environment without focusing directly on it. This includes noticing mood changes in others, monitoring 
food and drink consumption, observing people entering or leaving the room, and recognizing social 
interactions like whether a previously isolated person has started talking to someone—all while engaged 
in their own conversation.
 
Another form of awareness, situational awareness, involves understanding how current events and 
actions will impact future ones. This is particularly critical in high-stakes environments like air traffic 
control or an operating theater, where the flow of information and the ability to respond quickly is 
essential. In collaborative work settings, situational awareness helps individuals coordinate their actions, 
particularly in interdependent tasks where one person's work influences others. For example, in a theater 
production, performers constantly observe each other’s actions to ensure smooth coordination. 
A classic study by Heath and Luff (1992) examined the dynamic between two controllers in the London 
Underground control room. One controller (A) was responsible for train movements, while the other (B) 
communicated with passengers. Even without explicit verbal exchanges, controller B used peripheral 
awareness to monitor controller A’s actions and anticipate disruptions. For example, after overhearing A 
discuss a problem with a train driver, B proactively informed passengers of potential service delays. This 
study highlighted the importance of awareness and shared monitoring in effective coordination, 
especially in high-pressure environments.
