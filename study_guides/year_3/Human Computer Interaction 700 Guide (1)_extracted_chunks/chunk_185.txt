4.3.5 External Cognition  
 
People engage with and create information through various external representations such as books, web 
pages, sketches, diagrams, notes, drawings, and photos. Additionally, a wide array of tools, like pens, 
calculators, spreadsheets, and software workflows, has been developed over time to enhance cognition. 
The integration of these external representations and physical tools has significantly expanded and 
supported people's cognitive abilities. They have become such an essential part of our daily activities that 
it is hard to imagine functioning without them. 
 
The relationship between humans and technology has been theorized in various ways, with notable 
theories including external cognition (Scaife and Rogers, 1996) and the extended mind (Clark and 
Chalmers, 1998). The extended mind theory suggests that new digital technologies serve as extensions of 
our minds, while external cognition focuses on designing technologies and interfaces that enhance our 
cognitive abilities and reduce cognitive effort. Key concepts related to external cognition include: 

• Cognitive offloading to alleviate memory load 
• Computational offloading to reduce human cognitive effort in tasks 
• Annotating and cognitive tracing. 

Cognitive Offloading 
To reduce memory load, various strategies have been developed to externalize information that is hard 
to remember, such as birthdays, appointments, and addresses. Tools like diaries, personal reminders, and 
calendars serve as cognitive artifacts, helping people recall tasks at specific times, such as buying a 
birthday card for a relative. 
Other common external representations include notes like sticky notes, shopping lists, and to-do lists, 
often placed in prominent locations to ensure they are seen and remembered. People also place items in 
piles to indicate urgency, or rely on others, such as friends and family, to remind them of tasks. 

Cognitive offloading reduces memory burden by delegating reminders to external sources. This includes: 
• Reminding people to do something (e.g., buy a birthday card) 
• Helping them remember what to do (e.g., buy a card) 
• Alerting them about when to do something (e.g., send it by a certain date) 
Research shows that cognitive offloading is a common strategy to prevent forgetting and ease the effort 
of remembering. Confidence plays a role in this: the less confident someone is in their memory, the more 
likely they are to offload the task, regardless of how good their memory actually is (Gilbert et al., 2022). 
Technology has been designed to assist in this process, with apps like to-do lists and online calendars that 
send reminders, helping users manage time and maintain a healthy work-life balance.
