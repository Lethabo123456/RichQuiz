2.2.5 Four Basic Activities of Interaction Design 

The four key activities in interaction design are: 
• Discovering Requirements: This activity focuses on understanding the users and the needs that 
the interactive product should address. It involves gathering and analyzing data to define the 
product's requirements, which form the foundation for design and development. 
• Designing Alternatives: This is the main activity of the design phase, where ideas are generated 
to meet the identified requirements. It can be broken down into two parts: conceptual design and 
concrete design. Conceptual design involves creating a model that defines what users can do with 
the product and what concepts are needed to interact with it. Concrete design focuses on the 
detailed elements of the product, such as color, sounds, terminology, menu layouts, and icons. 
• Prototyping: Prototyping is a part of the design development phase. It involves creating models 
of the product to simulate interaction and appearance. Prototypes allow people to experience the 
design firsthand, which is crucial for evaluation. Prototyping doesn’t always require software; 
techniques like paper prototypes or role-playing can be used to quickly test and identify design 
issues.
• Evaluating: This activity corresponds to the Deliver phase of the design process. It involves testing 
the product’s usability and user experience based on established criteria. Evaluation is not a 
replacement for quality assurance or testing, but it complements these processes to ensure the 
product meets its intended purpose. 

These activities—discovering requirements, designing alternatives, prototyping, and evaluating—are 
interconnected. Prototypes help evaluate design alternatives, and feedback from evaluation informs 
further design or requirement refinement. In the double diamond process (Figure 2.1), this feedback is 
shown with dashed arrows, while the simple lifecycle diagram (Figure 2.4) uses solid arrows to indicate 
the same connections.
