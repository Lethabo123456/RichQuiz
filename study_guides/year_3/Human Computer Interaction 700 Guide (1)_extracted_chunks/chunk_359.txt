Best Practices for AgileUX 
1. User Research: Determine the type, scope, and timing of research activities to inform iterative 
cycles. 
2. Alignment with Agile: Adjust UX processes to accommodate agile characteristics, such as short 
timeframes and evolving priorities. 
3. Documentation: Balance the need for documentation with agile's preference for minimalism, 
focusing on delivering a functional product. 
Collaboration and Flexibility 
AgileUX thrives on cross-functional teamwork, where UX designers, engineers, and other specialists share 
tasks and insights. For instance: 

• Designers and engineers collaborate on tasks. 
• Engineers participate in user research. 
• Designers join daily stand-ups to align efforts. 
In conclusion, AgileUX requires UX designers to embrace flexibility, prioritize end-user outcomes over 
static artifacts, and foster a collaborative, iterative mindset to thrive in agile environments. 
 
9.2.1 User Research 
 
User research, also known as discovery research, involves collecting and analyzing data to understand 
potential users, their tasks, and the context in which they operate. While this research is typically 
conducted before product development begins, it remains valuable throughout the development process. 
In agile environments, time-intensive methods like ethnography are incompatible with short iteration 
cycles. As a result, technical development should proceed independently of such studies. Instead, agile 
projects benefit from focused activities, such as evaluating design components, clarifying requirements, 
or understanding task contexts, which can run concurrently with development. This is exemplified by the 
dual tracks approach. 

Bob Thomas (2021) recommends adopting lean user research methods, which involve team members 
from technical and design roles participating in usability tests, recording observations on sticky notes, and 
summarizing findings within hours or days rather than weeks. 
One strategy is to conduct user research before the project officially starts, often referred to as "iteration 
zero." This phase supports foundational activities, including software architecture design and user 
research. However, the time allotted for iteration zero may still be insufficient for more extensive 
research. Don Norman (2006) suggests conducting user research even earlier, before a project is 
announced, to avoid the time constraints imposed by agile timeboxes. 
Another approach involves maintaining a continuous user research program that regularly updates and 
enhances a company's understanding of its users over an extended period. This ongoing effort informs 
individual projects, with iteration zero focused on collecting project-specific data. For example, companies 
like Microsoft and Google recruit participants for various research activities, using the insights gained to 
guide product development.
