8.5.1 Questionnaire Structure   

Many questionnaires begin by requesting demographic information such as gender, ethnicity, age, and 
relevant experience details. This background data helps contextualize the responses, provided it aligns 
with the study's objectives. For example, differences in opinions may stem from varying levels of 
experience, such as between first-time users of a banking app and experienced users. However, certain 
details, like a participant's height, may not be relevant to their responses regarding social media use. 
Following the demographic section, specific questions related to the study's goals are typically asked. 
These may be grouped into related topics to make the questionnaire easier and more logical to complete. 

Here is a checklist for designing an effective questionnaire: 
• Consider the order of questions, as it can influence how participants respond. 
• Determine whether different versions of the questionnaire are needed for different populations. 
• Provide clear instructions, such as whether answers can be saved and completed later. Focus on 
clear wording and good typography. 
• Keep the questionnaire concise and focused on the study goals. 
• If the questionnaire is long, consider allowing respondents to skip sections. It's better to collect 
partial responses than none at all due to dropouts. 
• Pay attention to layout and pacing; balance white space and the use of individual web pages with 
the need to keep the questionnaire compact. 

8.5.2 Question and Response Format   
  
Questionnaires can be designed with various formats for both questions and responses. Closed-ended 
questions, where respondents select from a set of predefined answers, are common, often including 
options like "no opinion" or "none of these." In some cases, it's useful to ask respondents to provide 
answers within a specific range. Choosing the right question and response format helps participants 

answer clearly and makes it easier to analyze the results. Additionally, negatively worded questions 
should be used cautiously, and double negatives should be avoided, as they can be confusing and may 
result in inaccurate responses. 
 
Ranges and Predefined Lists of Responses  
Responses to certain questions can often be grouped within a predictable range or list. For example, 
nationality has a limited number of options, making it sensible to ask respondents to choose from a 
predefined list. A similar approach can be used for age, where respondents are typically asked to select 
an age range rather than a specific number. In some cases, multiple options can be chosen, such as 
selecting the news channels a participant regularly watches. For online surveys, single-choice questions 
often use radio buttons (see Figure 8.5(a)), while multiple-choice questions use checkboxes (see Figure 
8.5(b)). Alternatively, a drop-down menu may appear when the question is clicked (Figure 8.5(c)).

A common design issue arises when the ranges overlap. For instance, age ranges like 15–20 and 20–25 
create confusion, as a 20-year-old wouldn't know which box to select. Adjusting the ranges to 15–19 and 
20–24 resolves this issue. 
Another question that arises concerns whether all intervals should be equal in size. The answer depends 
on the research objectives. For example, if a website redesign project focuses on users under 26, the age 
ranges might be 17 and younger, 18–25, and 26 and older. Conversely, if analyzing political views across 
generations, broader ranges, such as 20 and younger, 21–30, 31–40, etc., may be more appropriate for 
respondents older than 21.
