2.3.2 How to Decide What to Design 

Deciding what to design is a crucial step in the interaction design process. One way to determine this is 
by exploring the problem space, a step often overlooked by newcomers to the field. When creating or 
modifying an interactive product, it's tempting to dive directly into the design of the physical interface 
and the technologies used. The issue with starting here is that it can lead to a misunderstanding of 
potential users and their context, overlook problems with the existing product, and ignore usability and 
user experience goals, as discussed in Chapter 1. 

For example, consider the augmented reality displays and holographic navigation systems in modern cars 
(see Figure 2.7). These systems are the result of extensive research into human factors, the driving 
experience, and the suitability of different technologies, as well as technological advancements. 
Understanding the problem space has been vital in developing solutions that are safe and reliable. While 
choosing the right technology and designing the physical interface are essential steps, it's best to make 
these decisions after thoroughly understanding the problem space. 

This means knowing what the current 
user experience is, why a change is necessary, and how the proposed changes will improve the 
experience. In the case of navigation systems, for instance, it's important to identify what issues exist with 
current systems, such as the distraction caused by small GPS displays while driving. 
Even when designing for a completely new interactive experience, understanding the context in which 
the product will be used is crucial. The process of defining the problem space usually involves the entire 
design team, with members offering different perspectives. For instance, a project manager will likely 
focus on budgets, timelines, and staffing, while a software engineer will consider the technical aspects of 
the design. It’s important to consider the implications of each perspective in relation to the others. 
Although this process can be time-consuming and sometimes lead to disagreements, its benefits are 
significant: it reduces the likelihood of incorrect assumptions and unsupported claims making their way 
into the design, which could result in a product that is unusable or unwanted. 
Taking time to reflect on and discuss ideas during the early stages of design opens up more possibilities 
and options. Additionally, designers are increasingly expected to justify their choices and present their 
reasoning in both business and design terms. The ability to think critically, analyze, and present a 
convincing argument is as highly valued as the ability to create a product (Kolko, 2011).
