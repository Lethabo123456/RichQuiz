3.6.2 Visions  
Visions are future scenarios that inspire research and development in interaction design, often portraying 
what life might look like with emerging technologies. One well-known vision is Mark Weiser's concept of 
ubiquitous computing, where technology is seamlessly integrated into everyday life. Other visions, such 
as Apple's Knowledge Navigator, have inspired the development of new technologies like Siri. While 
visions can drive innovation, they may not always predict the future accurately, and their limitations are 
often shaped by the time and cultural context in which they are created. 

3.6.3 Challenges 
Technological challenges are often framed within the context of global issues, such as climate change, 
sustainability, and equity. These challenges require HCI researchers to scale their methods and address 
large-scale problems, such as designing more sustainable products and technologies. For example, 
addressing the short lifespan of consumer electronics and advocating for design values that extend the 
life cycle of products. 

3.6.4 Theories 
Theories in human-computer interaction (HCI) are borrowed from various disciplines like cognitive 
psychology and sociology. These theories provide insights into how people interact with technology, 
shaping the design of user interfaces and experiences. Theories can be descriptive (identifying concepts), 
explanatory (understanding relationships), predictive (forecasting user behaviour), or prescriptive 
(offering design guidance). 

3.6.5 Models  
Models in interaction design simplify complex phenomena to help designers predict user behaviour and 
understand the underlying processes of human-computer interaction. For example, Don Norman's "seven 
stages of action" model, based on cognitive theory, explains how users plan and execute tasks when 
interacting with technology. Models often abstract from theories in disciplines like psychology to help 
designers structure their work.
 
3.6.6 Frameworks 
Frameworks are sets of concepts or questions that guide research and design within specific domains 
(e.g., collaborative learning) or methodologies (e.g., ethnographic studies). Frameworks provide 
structured approaches to designing and evaluating interactive products, often offering a way to categorize 
and analyze design challenges.
