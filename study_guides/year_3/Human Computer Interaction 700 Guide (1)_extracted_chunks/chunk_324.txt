The advantage of focus groups is that they bring out diverse viewpoints that may be overlooked in 
dividual interviews, particularly useful in collaborative processes or understanding different user stories. 
While a preset agenda guides the discussion, the facilitator allows flexibility for unexpected issues. The 
facilitator ensures balanced participation, encouraging quieter members to speak and preventing 
dominating voices. Discussions are typically recorded for later analysis, and participants may be asked to 
elaborate on their comments later. 

However, focus groups require careful facilitation to stay on track and avoid "groupthink," where opinions 
of a few participants can sway the entire group. They should not be the sole source of information about 
user behavior. The format of focus groups can be adapted to suit the participants and their context. For 
example, focus groups for older adults using smart home technology may be conducted within the smart 
home environment, and for the Mbeere community in Kenya, focus groups took the form of a traditional 
"talking circle" where each elder speaks in turn without interruption. 

8.4.5 Planning and Conducting an Interview   
Planning an interview involves several key steps: developing a set of questions or topics to be covered, 
preparing any documentation for the interviewee (such as consent forms and project descriptions), 
ensuring that recording software and equipment are functioning properly, structuring the interview, and 
arranging a suitable time and location. For in-person interviews, providing snacks and drinks can help 
create a relaxed and comfortable environment. 
 
Developing Interview Questions   
 
Here are some guidelines for developing interview questions (Robson and McCartan, 2016): 
• Avoid long or compound questions as they can be difficult to remember or confusing. Break them 
into separate questions. For example, instead of asking, “How do you like this smartphone app 
compared with previous ones that you have used?” ask, “How do you like this smartphone app?” 
and “Have you used other smartphone apps? If so, how did you like them?” This approach makes 
it easier for the interviewee to respond and for the interviewer to record. 
• Avoid using jargon or complex language that interviewees may not understand. Instead, explain 
things in clear and simple terms. 
• Keep questions neutral. For example, instead of asking, “Why do you like this style of interaction?” 
which assumes a positive response, ask in a way that allows the interviewee to express their true 
feelings without bias.
