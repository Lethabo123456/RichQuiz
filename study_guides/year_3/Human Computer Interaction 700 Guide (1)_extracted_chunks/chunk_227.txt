In many group meetings, some individuals tend to dominate the conversation while others contribute 
less, which is often acceptable in certain contexts but not always ideal. This raises the question of whether 
it's possible to design technologies that promote more equitable participation. Research has explored 
how shareable technologies can encourage more balanced interaction among participants. 
A key factor in achieving this balance is the design of the interface itself, specifically whether it allows 
participants to engage actively by selecting, adding, or manipulating content. A user study conducted by 
Rogers et al. (2009) demonstrated that using physical tokens on a table top display, which allowed 
participants to add digital content, led to more equal participation compared to interfaces that only used 
digital input through icons and menus. This design was particularly effective for individuals who might be 
shy or reluctant to speak up in a group. Those who normally spoke less ended up making more significant 
contributions to the task by interacting with the surface in terms of selecting, moving, or removing 
options. 

The study also showed how changing the mode of interaction can influence group dynamics, making it 
easier for more reticent participants to contribute without feeling pressure to speak. Additionally, real-
time feedback, such as ambient displays, has been used to enhance awareness of participation levels in 
groups. For example, LED indicators embedded in table tops or abstract visualizations on other devices 
can show how much each participant is talking, creating awareness of turn-taking. 
An example of this is the Reflect Table, which used microphones to monitor ongoing conversations and 
displayed the relative levels of participation via colored LEDs (Bachour et al., 2008). A study of this setup 
showed mixed results. Some participants adjusted their speaking behaviour to match others, while others 
ignored the feedback entirely. Those who spoke the most tended to reduce their contributions, while 
those who spoke the least were less likely to increase their participation. Interestingly, participants who 
valued equal contribution were more responsive to the LED feedback, while those who didn't prioritize 
equal participation were less likely to adjust their behaviour. 
This study highlights the complexity of balancing group participation and suggests that technology, such 
as ambient displays, can influence behaviour, but its effectiveness depends on individual attitudes and 
perceptions of the group dynamics. How would you respond to such feedback in a meeting, and would it 
influence your contribution?
