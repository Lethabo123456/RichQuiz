Chapter 9: INTERACTION DESIGN IN PRACTICE 
 
9.1 Introduction  
 
When situated within the broader context of commerce and business, interaction designers encounter 
various pressures, such as tight deadlines and constrained resources. They must also collaborate with 
individuals across diverse roles, beyond just stakeholders. Furthermore, the principles, techniques, and 
methodologies discussed in other chapters of this book must be translated into practical, real-world 
scenarios involving real people, which presents its own set of challenges. Harry Brignull, interviewed in 
Chapter 1, “What is Interaction Design?”, aptly remarked, “Research and design are naturally messy.” He 
further emphasized that interaction designers often step into roles that may initially feel outside their 
comfort zone while helping others grasp the user perspective. In essence, practicing interaction design 
involves navigating complexities and staying updated with evolving techniques and advancements. 
The role of an interaction design practitioner is known by various titles, including interface designer, 
information architect, experience designer, usability engineer, and user experience (UX) designer. This 
chapter primarily uses the terms UX designer and UX design, as these are the most commonly used in the 
industry to describe professionals responsible for tasks such as interface design, user evaluations, 
information architecture, visual design, persona development, and prototyping. However, interaction 
design practices can differ significantly between organizations. 
Pariya Kashfi et al. (2019), in their two-decade study of a software development company, highlighted 
that many organizations are transitioning from focusing solely on graphical user interfaces (GUIs) to 

adopting a broader UX perspective. This shift, however, is fraught with challenges, such as failing to 
distinguish UX from usability and conflicts between teams vying for control of UX practices. Additionally, 
they observed that companies now have a greater awareness of both internal and external stakeholders 
and their expectations compared to the past. 
While earlier chapters might suggest that designers work independently, relying primarily on stakeholders 
and close colleagues, in reality, UX designers draw support from several key areas. This chapter outlines 
four critical areas of support that shape the work of UX designers: 

1. Collaboration with Agile Teams: Working with software and product development teams that 
follow agile methodologies (discussed in Chapter 2, “The Process of Interaction Design”) has driven 
the adaptation of techniques and processes, leading to AgileUX approaches. 
2. Leveraging Existing Designs and Concepts: Utilizing interaction and UX design patterns allows 
designers to build on proven solutions, saving time and avoiding the need to “reinvent the wheel.” 
3. Using Reusable Components: Components such as screen widgets, source code libraries, motors, 
sensors, and even complete robots can be modified and integrated into prototypes or final 
products. While design patterns embody conceptual interaction ideas, reusable components 
provide tangible, implemented building blocks. 
4. Employing Specialized Tools and Environments: Designers have access to a variety of tools and 
platforms that support tasks such as creating visual designs, wireframes, interface sketches, and 
interactive prototypes. 
These support systems, combined with the ability to adapt to dynamic environments, are crucial for the 
success of UX designers in practice.
