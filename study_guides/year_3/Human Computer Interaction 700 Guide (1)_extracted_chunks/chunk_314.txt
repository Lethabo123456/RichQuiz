8.3.2 Audio Plus Photographs   

Audio recording is a valuable alternative to note-taking and is generally less intrusive than video. During 
observations, it enables the observer to focus on the activity rather than attempting to capture every 
spoken word. In interviews, it allows the interviewer to concentrate on the interviewee without the 
distraction of taking notes. Full transcription of the recordings is not always necessary; often, only specific 
sections are required based on the study's goals. Many studies don't require extensive detail, and 
recordings can serve as reminders or as sources of anecdotes for reports. It's surprising how powerful 
audio recordings of people or locations from the session can be, providing additional context for the 
analysis. 

When audio recording is the primary or sole data collection method, the recording quality must be high. 
This typically involves ensuring that the recording device is placed away from loud machinery or air 
conditioning units and testing connections and acoustics when gathering data remotely. Many 
videoconferencing platforms like Zoom and Teams offer built-in recording capabilities and may even 
generate transcriptions, either as live captions displayed in a side panel or post-recording transcriptions. 
While the accuracy of these transcriptions is usually around 80–90%, it can be affected by background 
noise and the clarity of the speaker's voice. Automated transcription has significantly simplified the 
process for researchers, eliminating the need for manual transcription. Audio recordings are often paired 
with photographs to further enrich the data. 

8.3.3 Video   
 
Smartphones can capture short video clips of activities, offering good-quality output, but for long-
duration recordings or situations where the researcher can't be present, dedicated recording devices may 
be better. These devices provide more recording options and prevent the researcher from needing to 
hold the phone. When choosing and positioning a camera, several factors need consideration: 

1. Choosing the Camera: Options include wearable body cameras, 360-degree cameras, and 
standard camcorders. The choice depends on the study's focus—wearable cameras capture the 
participant’s perspective, while 360-degree cameras provide a full context. 
2. Fixed or Flexible Settings: Cameras can be either fixed in one position or adjustable for zoom and 
focus. This decision depends on whether the researcher will manage the camera or leave it to 
record automatically. 
3. Camera Placement: Properly planning where to place cameras is crucial. In studies conducted in 
real-world settings, it’s helpful to observe the environment briefly before recording. Involving 
participants in the setup can help ensure relevant actions are captured, especially in private 
settings like homes. 
4. Impact on Participants: Video recording may influence participants' behavior, as they might act 
differently when aware of being filmed. It's important to empirically examine the data to 
determine if participants alter their behavior due to the camera's presence.
