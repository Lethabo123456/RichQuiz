2.3 Some Practical Issues  
 
The discussion so far has highlighted some issues about the practical application of people centered 
design and the simple lifecycle of interaction design introduced earlier. These issues are listed here:  
•  How to find out what people need  
•  How to decide what to design  
•  How to generate alternative designs  
•  How to choose among alternatives  
•  How to integrate interaction design activities with other lifecycle models  
 
2.3.1 How to Find Out What People Need  
 
If you had asked someone on the street in the late 1990s what they needed, they likely wouldn't have 
mentioned a smart TV, a ski jacket with an integrated smartphone, or a robotic pet. However, if you had 
shown them these possibilities and asked if they would buy them, they might have responded more 
positively. Determining what product to create isn't simply about asking people "What do you need?" and 
providing it, as people often don’t know what is possible. Suzanne and James Robertson (2013) refer to 
these as "un-dreamed-of" needs—needs people are unaware they have. Instead of directly asking people, 
the approach should involve exploring the problem space, understanding potential users, their context, 
and activities, and identifying areas for improvement. Additionally, experimenting with ideas can help 
uncover what would truly make a difference. 

In practice, a combination of these approaches is often employed: trying out ideas to discover 
requirements and determine what to build, based on a deep understanding of the problem space, 
potential users, stakeholders, and their activities. Studies in real-world settings or rapid design sprints, 
which provide real user feedback on early concepts, are particularly valuable for new inventions. Instead 
of speculating on who might use a product and what they might do with it, it's more effective to introduce 
the product and observe—the findings can often be unexpected. Many designers and developers have 
remarked that it’s an “eye-opening experience” when they see users struggle with tasks that seemed clear 
to them. Focusing on people’s goals, usability goals, and user experience goals offers a more effective approach to 
interaction design than simply expecting stakeholders to articulate product requirements.
