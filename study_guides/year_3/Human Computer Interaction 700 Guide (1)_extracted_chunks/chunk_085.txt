2.2.4 What Is a People-Centered Approach?  
 
Several decades ago, when the field of human-computer interaction (HCI) was emerging, John Gould and 
Clayton Lewis (1985) established three key principles they believed would lead to the development of a 
"useful and easy-to-use computer system." These principles are: 

• Early focus on users and tasks: This principle emphasizes the importance of first understanding 
the users by studying their cognitive, behavioral, physical, and attitudinal traits. This involves 
observing users as they perform their everyday tasks, analyzing those tasks, and involving users in 
the design process from the outset. 
• Empirical measurement: Early in the development process, it is crucial to observe and measure 
the reactions and performance of target users when they interact with materials such as printed 
scenarios and manuals. As development progresses, users interact with simulations and 
prototypes, and their reactions and performance are continuously observed, recorded, and 
analyzed. 
• Iterative design: This principle involves addressing problems found during user testing and then 
conducting further tests to evaluate the impact of changes. It emphasizes an iterative cycle of 
design, testing, measuring, and redesigning, which is repeated until an effective solution is 
achieved. 

These principles are the foundation of a user-centered design approach. When they were first introduced, 
however, they were not widely adopted or understood.
