1.4 What to Design  
 
Designing interactive products requires careful consideration of the people who will use them, how they 
will be used, and the context in which they will be used. Understanding the activities users are engaged 
in when interacting with these products is also crucial. The type of interface and the arrangement of input 
and output devices should align with the activities being supported. For instance, when designing an 
online banking interface, it must be secure, trustworthy, and easy to navigate, while also providing non-
intrusive access to information about new services. The design must prioritize functionality and user 
experience to effectively meet the needs of the user. 

There is a growing variety of interfaces and interactive devices, ranging from multitouch displays and 
speech-based systems to mobile devices and wearables. These devices can be designed with different 
interaction methods, such as menus, icons, gestures, or commands, to suit the context of use. New 
innovations, like wearable glasses for augmented reality, and everyday items such as smart cameras, 
microwaves, and washing machines, are increasingly incorporating digital interfaces. Self-checkout 
systems at grocery stores, libraries, and airports have become common, reflecting the shift toward digital 
interaction in everyday life. More recently, AI-powered smart supermarkets, like Amazon Go, use 
advanced computer vision, sensors, and deep learning to track customers' purchases automatically, 
eliminating the need for manual checkouts.
