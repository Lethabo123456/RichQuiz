Memory Load and Passwords   

Phone, online, and mobile banking offer customers the convenience of conducting financial transactions, 
such as bill payments and balance checks, at their own convenience. However, these platforms face 
challenges in managing security concerns, particularly preventing fraudulent transactions like bank scams, 
where customers are tricked into transferring money. 

To address these security concerns, banks often require customers to provide multiple pieces of personal 
information before granting access to their accounts. Common examples include answers to security 
questions such as their ZIP code, mother's maiden name, and birthplace, or providing a memorable 
address and date. While some of these questions are easy to recall, others, like providing a specific letter 
from a password, can be more difficult, especially when banks ask for letters from specific positions in the 
password. This process can increase cognitive load, making it hard for customers to recall the correct 
information quickly. 

To enhance security, banks also randomize the security questions, preventing anyone nearby from 
memorizing the sequence. However, this increases the memory burden on customers, who may resort to 
writing down their security details, which could compromise security if someone gains access to the 
written information. 

Multifactor authentication (MFA) has become standard, particularly for authorizing online payments. 
Typically, a password is required first, followed by a code sent to another device (e.g., a mobile phone) or 
email. While this step reduces fraud risk, it adds complexity and can be inconvenient if the user doesn't 
have their devices on hand. 
To ease the memory burden, password managers like LastPass have been developed, allowing users to 
store and manage all their passwords securely, requiring them to remember only one main password. 

4.2.4 Learning  
Learning is closely linked to memory, as it involves acquiring skills and knowledge that rely on memory for 
retention. Without memory, people would not be able to retain what they learn. Cognitive psychology 
distinguishes between incidental and intentional learning. Incidental learning happens naturally, without 
intention, such as recognizing faces or recalling events of the day. Intentional learning, on the other hand, 
is goal-driven, such as studying for an exam or learning a new language, and often requires conscious 
effort. 
For software developers, this highlights the importance of not assuming users will effortlessly learn to use 
an app or product; intentional learning is often involved. Moreover, people typically find it difficult to 
learn by reading manuals; they prefer hands-on learning. Graphical user interfaces (GUIs) and direct 

manipulation interfaces support this type of active learning by allowing users to interact and undo actions, 
which is especially helpful when mistakes occur. 
Various technologies, such as online learning platforms, AI tutors, and virtual reality (VR), have been 
developed to enhance intentional learning. These tools offer new ways to interact with information that 
traditional methods like books cannot. For example, interactive simulations, wearables, and augmented 
reality (AR) have been used to teach abstract concepts like mathematical formulas and physics laws. A 
study by Su Cai et al. (2021) demonstrated how integrating AR into physics classrooms improved students' 
self-efficacy and motivation, as the technology helped make complex physics phenomena clearer through 
enhanced visual cues.
