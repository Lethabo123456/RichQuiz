Other theories suggest emotions do drive behaviors—for example, fear triggering flight or anger 
prompting a fight response. Evolutionary psychology supports this view, explaining that emotional 
responses to fear or anger focus attention on resolving perceived dangers. These emotions often activate 
physiological changes like adrenaline surges, muscle tension, sweating, rapid breathing, and even nausea. 
While these responses prepare the body for action, they can also cause discomfort. 
A common example of emotion-driven nervousness is stage fright, where fear and apprehension occur 
before public speaking or performing. Andreas Komninos (2017) explains this as the autonomic nervous 
system signaling people to avoid potentially embarrassing situations. However, performers and speakers 
must face their fears, often channeling adrenaline into focus and energy. For some, this nervous state 
enhances performance, while others simply endure it until they can relax afterward. 

Emotions vary in complexity and duration. Automatic emotions (or affect) are quick, reflexive responses 
that dissipate rapidly, while conscious emotions develop and fade more slowly. The latter often result 
from deliberate cognitive processes, such as reflection or decision-making. Recognizing these distinctions 
helps us better understand how emotions influence human behavior and interactions.  
Understanding emotions provides valuable insights into designing user experiences that evoke affect or 
reflection. Don Norman (2005) highlights that a positive emotional state can enhance creativity by 
reducing focus, making it easier for people to generate ideas and make decisions quickly. When users are 
in a good mood, they tend to cope better with minor issues in a device or interface. In contrast, anxious 
or angry users are less tolerant, requiring interfaces to be highly visible and provide unambiguous 
feedback. Norman emphasizes the importance of detail-oriented design for systems used in stressful 
situations, stating, “things intended to be used under stressful situations require a lot more care, with 
much more attention to detail” (Norman, 2005, p. 26). 
Anthony Ortony, Don Norman, and William Revelle (2005) proposed a three-level model of emotion and 
behavior based on brain processes. 

1. Visceral Level: This is the most basic level, where the brain responds automatically and rapidly to 
external stimuli. It evaluates whether something is good, bad, safe, or dangerous, triggering 
immediate emotional and physiological responses such as fear, joy, or anger. For instance, seeing 
a large spider may elicit fear, prompting a scream and an instinctive reaction to flee. 
2. Behavioral Level: This level involves well-practiced, everyday activities such as typing, speaking, 
or swimming. These behaviors are largely routine and operate without conscious thought. 
3. Reflective Level: The highest level involves conscious thought, reflection, and generalization 
beyond immediate experiences. For example, while watching a horror movie, a person might 
consciously analyze its narrative structure or special effects (reflective level) while simultaneously 
feeling fear during intense scenes (visceral level). 
This model illustrates how different brain processes work together, enabling designers to create user 
experiences that account for the interplay of automatic reactions, learned behaviors, and reflective 
thinking.
