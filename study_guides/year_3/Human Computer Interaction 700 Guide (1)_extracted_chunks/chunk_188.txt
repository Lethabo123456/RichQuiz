Computational Offloading  
Computational offloading refers to using a tool or device alongside an external representation to assist in 
performing a calculation. For instance, solving a math problem using pen and paper is an example of 
computational offloading. To demonstrate this, consider multiplying 21 × 19 in your head versus using 
pen and paper. Now, try the same multiplication using Roman numerals: XXI × XVIIII. The task becomes 
significantly more difficult unless you are skilled in Roman numerals, even though the problem itself 
remains the same. This is because different representations transform the difficulty of the task, and the 
type of tool used can either simplify or complicate the task. 
Annotating and Cognitive Tracing 
One way we externalize our cognition is by modifying representations to reflect changes we want to keep 
track of. For example, people often mark off tasks on a to-do list to show completed items or rearrange 
objects in their environment as tasks evolve. These actions fall under two categories: annotating and 
cognitive tracing. 
• Annotating involves altering external representations, such as crossing off or underlining items. 
For instance, when shopping, people often create a list after reviewing their pantry. As they shop, 
they cross off items, providing a quick visual representation of what’s left to buy. 
• Cognitive tracing involves changing the order or structure of items externally. This is useful when 
a situation is dynamic, such as during a game. For example, in card games, rearranging cards can 
help determine the best strategy. Similarly, in Scrabble, rearranging letters helps find optimal word 
combinations. 
In digital contexts, annotation tools allow users to mark or note changes directly on documents, storing 
these modifications for later review. Cognitive tracing is also applied in interactive environments, such as 
in online learning tools, where progress is visually represented. 
The general principle for interaction design based on external cognition is to provide representations that 
minimize memory load and cognitive effort. Tools like information visualizations and interactive interfaces 
help reduce the mental effort needed to analyse complex data, enabling users to perform tasks they 
otherwise couldn’t, like identifying patterns or anomalies in large datasets.
