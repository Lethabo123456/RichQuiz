3.2 Conceptualizing Interaction 
 
When starting a design project, it's crucial to clearly define the underlying assumptions and claims. An 
assumption refers to something that is taken for granted but requires further exploration—such as the 
belief that people desire fully automated cars. A claim, on the other hand, involves stating something as 
true, though it remains open to question—like the idea that people will feel safe and enjoy being driven 
by a car instead of driving it themselves. The key difference is that assumptions relate to current issues or 
conditions, while claims are projections about future outcomes resulting from a particular action or 
product introduction.

Documenting your assumptions and claims, and then attempting to defend and support them, can help 
reveal those that are unclear or unsupported. This process allows poorly formulated design ideas to be 
reworked. In many projects, it involves identifying problematic human activities and determining how 
they can be improved by introducing a different set of functions. In other cases, it may require more 
speculative thinking, imagining how to create an engaging user experience that does not yet exist. Box 
3.1 presents a hypothetical scenario where a team works through their assumptions and claims, 
illustrating how this approach helps clarify and explore problems. 

Sharing assumptions and claims about why a particular idea might be good (or not) allows the design team 
to gain diverse perspectives on the problem space. This process helps uncover conflicting or problematic 
views. The following framework offers a set of core questions to guide design teams in this exploration: 
• Are there issues with the current product or user experience? If so, what are they? 
• Why do you believe these problems exist? 
• What evidence supports the presence of these issues? 
• How do you think your proposed design ideas can address these problems? 
 
It is essential to clarify one’s assumptions about an identified problem or new opportunity, as well as the 
claims made about potential solutions, early in the project and throughout the process. Design teams 
must also determine the best way to conceptualize the design space. This primarily involves expressing 
the proposed solution as a conceptual model in terms of the user experience.
