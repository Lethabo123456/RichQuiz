Running the Interview   
 
Before beginning the interview, it is crucial to ensure that the interviewee has received and reviewed any 
project information sheet and completed the informed consent form. The interviewee should be given an 
opportunity to ask any questions they may have about the process. This can be addressed through email 
prior to the interview day or confirmed at the start of the interview. During the interview, the interviewer 
should prioritize listening over speaking, respond empathetically without bias, and appreciate the 
exchange. A typical interview sequence follows this structure (Robson and McCartan, 2016): 

1. Introduction: The interviewer introduces themselves and explains the purpose of the interview. 
Documentation is checked, and the interviewee has a chance to ask questions and consent to 
being recorded. This should be consistent for each interviewee. 
2. Warm-up: The interviewer begins with simple questions, possibly about demographic 
information, such as, “What area of the country do you live in?” 
3. Main session: The questions are presented in a logical order, with more probing questions towards 
the end. In a semi-structured interview, the question sequence may vary depending on the flow 
of the conversation, the depth of probing, and what feels more natural. 
4. Cooling-off: A few simple questions like, “Is there anything else you’d like to tell us?” help wind 
down the interview. 
5. Closing: The interviewer thanks the interviewee, ends any recordings, and signals that the 
interview is complete. 

8.4.6 Doing Interviews Remotely   
 
Remote interviews have become increasingly common, and with the availability of high-quality video-
conferencing systems, they offer a viable alternative to in-person interactions. Some advantages of 
remote focus groups and interviews include: 

• Participants are in a familiar environment, which makes them feel more at ease. 
• No need for participants to travel or worry about health and safety concerns. 
• Participants don’t need to worry about their attire. 
• For interviews on sensitive topics, interviewees can remain anonymous, especially if only audio is 
used. 

Additionally, participants can easily leave the conversation by disconnecting, which enhances their sense 
of security. From the interviewer's perspective, remote interviews allow access to a broader range of 
participants. However, potential drawbacks include the interviewer not being able to observe 
participants' body language clearly, and participants might be distracted and multitask during the session.

Running remote focus groups presents additional challenges, particularly when managing participation 
from multiple people. To engage participants effectively, a combination of digital tools may be used. For 
instance, a videoconferencing system like Zoom or Teams can be paired with collaborative tools for 
activities such as brainstorming, mind mapping, and diagramming. Tools like Miro, Mural, and Jamboard 
are commonly used alongside these platforms. Organizing the focus group around these collaborative 
activities helps foster a dynamic atmosphere. However, facilitating such online sessions can be 
exhausting, so shorter sessions may be more manageable.
