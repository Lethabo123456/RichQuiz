8.2.5 Triangulation   
Triangulation refers to studying a phenomenon from at least two different perspectives (Denzin, 2006; 
Jupp, 2006), and four types have been defined by Jupp (2006): 

1. Data Triangulation involves gathering data from various sources, times, locations, or individuals, 
potentially using different sampling techniques. 
2. Investigator Triangulation refers to involving multiple researchers in the collection and 
interpretation of data, such as different observers or interviewers. 
3. Theory Triangulation uses multiple theoretical frameworks to interpret the data or findings. 
4. Methodological Triangulation employs different data collection techniques. 
The most common form of triangulation is methodological triangulation, which validates findings by 
pointing to similar results achieved through different perspectives. However, achieving true triangulation 
is challenging because various data collection methods generate different types of data that may not 
always be compatible. Similarly, using different theoretical frameworks may not necessarily yield 
complementary results, and for theoretical triangulation to be effective, the frameworks must share 
similar philosophical foundations. While true triangulation is difficult, combining multiple data collection 
techniques and analysis approaches often provides valuable insights. 
Another type of triangulation focuses on data verification and reliability, known as "ground truth" 
validation. This is commonly applied in studies involving large datasets, such as crowdsourcing and 
machine learning, to ensure the authenticity and reliability of the data. However, identifying ground truth 
can be complex. In some fields, self-reported or human-labeled data is considered ground truth, but the 
accuracy of such labeling can be questionable. For example, Nan Gao et al. (2021) explored the reliability 
of self-reported data for determining mental states, often used as ground truth in machine learning 
models. Their research found discrepancies between physiologically measured and perceived 
engagement, highlighting the challenges in determining ground truth.

8.2.6 Pilot Studies   
A pilot study is a small-scale trial run of the main study designed to identify potential issues in advance, 
allowing researchers to correct them before the full study is conducted. It often involves a limited number 
of participants and may include testing equipment, instructions, interview questions, or questionnaires 
for clarity, as well as confirming experimental procedures. The aim is also to verify that the expected data 
can be collected as planned. 
Pilot studies are commonly used in qualitative research, but their results are often not published 
(Malmqvist et al., 2019), which can make their use less transparent. However, Omid Mohaddesi and 
Casper Harteveld (2020) report the results of a pilot study investigating the use of game-based systems 
to study human decision-making. Their pilot was not just a simplified version of the main study but 
focused on evaluating the gaming environment itself to determine if it was suitable for future 
experiments. They addressed specific questions, such as whether disruptions impacted user behavior, 
how varying amounts of information affected behavior, and whether participants engaged with all game 
interface elements. 
If access to participants is challenging, researchers may invite colleagues or peers to participate in the 
pilot study. However, anyone who takes part in a pilot study cannot participate in the main study. This is 
because prior involvement in the pilot could lead to biased results, as participants would already have 
knowledge of the study’s purpose and procedures.
