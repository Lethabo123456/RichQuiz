4.3.2 Gulfs of Execution and Evaluation  
 
The concept of the "gulf of execution" and the "gulf of evaluation" was an early influential framework in 
Human-Computer Interaction (HCI). It outlined the gaps that exist between the user and the interface, 
focusing on how designs can bridge these gaps to reduce cognitive effort. The gulf of execution represents 
the distance from the user to the physical system, while the gulf of evaluation refers to the distance from 
the system back to the user. Designers are challenged to bridge these gaps to make tasks easier for users. 
This concept helped in exploring the potential mismatches between a system's design and how users 
understand and interact with it. 

4.3.3 Information Processing   
 
One approach to understanding cognitive processes involves using metaphors and analogies. Various 
comparisons have been made, such as conceptualizing the mind as a reservoir, a telephone network, a 
digital computer, or a deep learning network. A popular metaphor in the 1980s was the idea of the mind 
as an information processor. In this view, information enters and exits the mind through a series of 
ordered processing stages, with different processes, like comparison and response selection, acting upon 
mental representations. These representations are thought to include images, mental models, rules, and 
other forms of knowledge.  

The information processing model helped make predictions about human performance, including 
reaction times and potential bottlenecks caused by information overload. One early model based on this 
theory was the human processor model, which described the cognitive processes involved when a user 
interacts with a computer (Card et al., 1983). It viewed cognition as a series of stages, where perceptual, 
cognitive, and motor processes were interconnected, predicting the time taken for users to perform 
various tasks. Although this model was useful in the 1980s for comparing word processors, it has since 
been largely replaced by more modern models that focus on understanding cognition within its real-world 
context. Today, the focus is on how external structures, such as the environment, can assist cognition and 
reduce cognitive load, as seen in models of distributed cognition, external cognition, and embodied 
cognition.
