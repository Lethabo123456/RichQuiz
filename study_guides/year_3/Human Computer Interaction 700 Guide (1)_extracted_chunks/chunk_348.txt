8.7.2 Adapting Techniques for Different Participants  
 
Technology developers must tailor their data gathering techniques to suit the specific participants and 
the context of the study. For instance: 
• Working with children: Children think and respond differently from adults, so data gathering 
methods need to be adapted to make them comfortable. For example, Cynthia Putnam et al. 
(2020) developed a visual Likert scale for children aged 7-11 years. For younger children, especially 
those in the pre-reading or early reading stages, it's better to use images, play, and interactive 
activities rather than written questionnaires. 
• Working with people with intellectual disabilities: In studies involving individuals with intellectual 
disabilities, Laurianne Sitbon and Shanjana Farhin (2017) suggest involving caregivers who can 
help make questions more concrete and understandable. For example, caregivers can explain 
concepts that participants might not easily grasp, such as the idea of a phone app providing bus 
information. 
Adapting methods to participants' specific needs ensures that data gathering is effective and meaningful 
for the group being studied. 

8.7.3 Gathering Data Remotely   
 
Gathering data remotely can provide access to a more diverse set of participants compared to face-to-
face methods. This approach is especially valuable for engaging people from different countries, age 
groups, or with varying abilities. As remote data collection becomes more common, it sometimes 
necessitates adjustments, such as transitioning from direct observation to indirect observation, where 
participants' activities are video-recorded and analyzed later rather than observed in real-time. 
During the COVID-19 pandemic, many studies had to adapt to remote data gathering, which led to 
valuable insights and practices. For example, Angela Mastrianni et al. (2021) shared their experiences of 
conducting participatory design workshops, usability evaluations, and interviews remotely, specifically in 
the emergency medical field. Their findings emphasized that remote user-centered design activities are 
feasible with some creative problem-solving. They also proposed best practices for conducting such 
activities remotely, helping researchers ensure effective data collection even in challenging 
circumstances.
