8.2.3 Relationship with Participants   
 
A crucial element of data gathering is the relationship between the researchers and the participants. 
Establishing a clear, professional rapport fosters trust and increases the likelihood of a successful study. 
In many countries, participants must receive sufficient information about the study's purpose, the type 
of data being collected, and its intended use. This ensures they can make an informed decision about their 
participation, a process referred to as informed consent. Evidence of consent must be documented, either 
through a signed form or an audio/video recording. 

A typical written consent form (e.g., in the U.S. or U.K.) asks participants to confirm their understanding 
of the study's purpose, the use of their data, and their voluntary agreement to participate. It typically 
includes assurances of data privacy and secure storage, and it often specifies participants’ right to 
withdraw at any time, with the guarantee that their data will not be used in such cases. When data 
collection involves recordings, oral consent may be appropriate. Researchers in some institutions may 
also need ethics training and approval from an ethics board before beginning their study. 

The principle of informed consent benefits both parties. For researchers, it ensures the collected data can 
be used in analysis, presentations, and publications. For participants, it provides reassurance that their 
data will not be misused or used against their interests, which is especially critical for vulnerable groups 
like children or individuals with disabilities. In these cases, parental consent may be required, and 
additional measures, such as non-disclosure agreements, may apply when working with professionals in 
commercial contexts. The specific approach to obtaining informed consent should be tailored to the 
participants and the situation, emphasizing ethical considerations and informed judgment. 
Building rapport is essential for encouraging relaxed and honest participation. However, in remote 
settings, this can be challenging, particularly when communication is limited (e.g., audio-only 
interactions), as the absence of body language and personal dynamics can slow the process of establishing 
trust (Dray & Siegel, 2004). 

Effective strategies for building rapport may vary based on the context. For 
example, Kaulbach et al. (2021) documented the importance of engaging with the Donkerbos San 
community in Namibia through culturally sensitive communication practices. These included meetings 
with the local headman, community gatherings to share project updates, and structured departure 
meetings. Such approaches helped build trust and demonstrated respect for sociocultural norms. 
Incentives can play a significant role in recruiting participants, particularly when there is no immediate 
benefit for them. For instance, asking sales executives to complete a questionnaire about a new app 
affecting their work requires a different motivational approach compared to asking schoolchildren to test 

a new game. The nature of the study and the participants’ motivations should inform the type and scale 
of incentives offered to ensure adequate participation.
