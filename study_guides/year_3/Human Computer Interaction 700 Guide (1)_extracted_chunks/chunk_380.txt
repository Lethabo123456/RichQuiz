9.5 Tools for Interaction Design  
 
The range and complexity of digital tools available to support UX designers have expanded greatly in 
recent years. The role of UX in business and the tools supporting it are continuously evolving (MacDonald 
et al., 2022). These tools assist with various aspects of the design process, including creative thinking, 
collaboration, design sketching, prototyping, simulation, evaluation, pattern library searches, and mind 
mapping. Nearly every stage of the design process has a corresponding tool. For instance, Miro and Mural 
are used for collaboration and brainstorming, Sketch is ideal for creating drawings and screen layouts, 
Balsamiq aids in wireframing, Overflow.io helps in generating playable user flow diagrams, and 
UXarchive.com provides a vast collection of reusable user flows. 
With the rise in popularity of design systems, many tools now combine various features in one platform, 
such as brainstorming, prototyping, wireframing, and UI design kits with code snippets and patterns. For 
example, Figma (figma.com) facilitates a range of collaborative design activities, including wireframing 
and prototyping, while Adobe XD (www.adobe.com/products/xd) allows for design, layout, animation, 
and voice prototyping. Some of the most widely used tools are available as open source or offer free trials, 
making it worth exploring their individual features. Other commonly utilized tools include Balsamiq 
(balsamiq.com), Axure RP (www.axure.com), and Sketch (sketchapp.com). 

Throughout this book, we have highlighted the importance of low-fidelity prototyping and its role in 
obtaining user feedback. However, like any prototype, paper-based prototypes have their limitations, 
particularly in not supporting user-driven interaction. To address this, research has long focused on 
developing interactive, low-fidelity prototypes (e.g., Segura et al., 2012), with recent advancements 
exploring neural networking approaches (Suleri et al., 2019). The goal is to generate mid- and high-fidelity 
prototypes from low-fidelity sketches, utilizing existing patterns, frameworks, and user flows. 
While tools for visual and interactive products, particularly apps for smartphones, desktops, and mobiles, 
are well-established, there is a lack of tools to support the development of other interfaces, such as brain, 
holographic, or virtual reality interfaces, as discussed in Chapter 7, "Interfaces." At present, most of the 
tools for these types of interfaces are still in the research phase. For instance, George Mo et al. (2021) 
describe a tool designed to aid in the creation of hand gesture recognizers for mixed reality applications. 
Despite the crowded marketplace for UX design tools, there remains significant potential for new 
developments that will influence UX design practices.
