4.3.4 Distributed Cognition  
 
Cognitive activities often involve interactions with external representations like books, documents, 
computers, and other people. For instance, when returning home, individuals rely on environmental cues, 
such as recognizing landmarks or intersections, rather than remembering every detail of the route. At 
home, they don’t need to recall the exact location of items, as the environment provides cues when 
needed. Similarly, people create external representations to reduce memory load, share information, and 
enhance their cognitive abilities (Kirsh, 2010). 

The distributed cognition approach was developed to explore cognitive processes across individuals, 
artifacts, and both internal and external representations (Hutchins, 1995). This approach describes a 
cognitive system, which includes interactions between people, tools, and the environment. For example, 
in an airline cockpit, the goal is to fly the plane, involving interactions between the pilot, captain, air traffic 
controller, and various instruments. The distributed cognition approach aims to understand how 
information flows across different media, like spoken words, instruments, or maps, and how it transforms 
during these exchanges (known as changes in representational state). Analysing this information flow 
helps when redesigning aspects of a cognitive system, such as improving a display or social practices, 
ensuring that shared awareness and synchronization are maintained, as seen in fields like healthcare and 
software team interactions.
