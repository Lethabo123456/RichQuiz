Designing technologies to encourage more equitable participation in collaborative tasks, especially 
around shared table tops, presents challenges. While providing real-time feedback about how much 
someone is speaking can make participants aware of their involvement, it may also be intimidating for 
those who are speaking less. Those who find it difficult to contribute verbally—such as individuals who 
are shy, non-native speakers, or those with conditions like stuttering or autism—may find these types of 
feedback discouraging. For them, providing discreet and accessible ways to add or manipulate content 
during a group task may encourage more participation without the pressure of verbal communication. 
Research on enhancing awareness has mainly focused on visual cues, but this approach doesn't always 
address the needs of people with visual impairments, such as those who are blind or have low vision. 
These individuals often face particular challenges in social interactions, especially when it comes to 
remembering who is present in a co-located group setting. 

To address this, researchers like Cecily Morrison and colleagues (2021) developed People Lens, a wearable 
technology designed to help blind people engage more effectively in social settings. The device uses 
augmented reality and computer vision algorithms to track and identify individuals nearby, capturing their 
gaze direction. It then provides this information to the wearer through spatialized audio, making it seem 
as though the sound is coming from the direction of the person. This technology enhances the wearer’s 
sense of awareness and facilitates social interactions by helping them identify who is present and 
understand where others are looking or engaging. This type of innovation highlights the potential for 
designing technologies that cater to a diverse range of users, ensuring that everyone has equal 
opportunities to participate in collaborative settings.
