In a people-centered design approach, these principles are further expanded to include the following: 

• Tasks and goals drive development: While technology can influence design decisions, it should 
not be the primary driver. Instead of focusing on how to deploy new technology, the focus should 
be on what technologies are available to better support people's goals. 
• Behavior and context of use are studied: It’s not only important to understand the tasks and goals 
of users, but also how they perform these tasks. Studying user behavior helps reveal priorities, 
preferences, and implicit intentions, which inform design decisions. 
• Characteristics of people are considered: People often blame themselves when technology fails, 
but technology should be designed to accommodate human limitations. Cognitive limitations (e.g., 
attention, memory, perception) and physical limitations (e.g., height, mobility, strength) should 
be accounted for in design. Some limitations are general, like color blindness, while others are 
specific to certain tasks or professions. 
• Consulting users and stakeholders throughout development: Users and other stakeholders 
should be involved in the design process from the early phases to the final stages. There are 
various ways to involve users and determine the level of their participation. 
• Design decisions are made within the context of use: All design choices should take into account 
the users’ activities, goals, and environment. This may include involving users in design decisions, 
with co-creation being one way to do this. 
• Clear identification of usability and user experience goals: At the start of the project, specific 
usability and user experience goals should be defined, documented, and agreed upon. These goals 
help guide the design process, assist in making decisions between alternative designs, and allow 
progress to be measured throughout the development. By setting measurable goals upfront, the 
product can be evaluated empirically at different stages of development. 
• Iteration through data collection, idea generation, design development, and evaluation: 
Iteration is key for gathering feedback and refining ideas. As designers and stakeholders engage in 
discussions about requirements, needs, and aspirations, new insights will emerge about what is 
necessary, helpful, and feasible. This process naturally requires iteration, as each cycle informs the 
next. Innovation is rarely immediate; it requires time, evolution, trial and error, and patience. 
Designers often don’t get the solution right on the first attempt.
