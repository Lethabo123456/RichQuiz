6.6 Anthropomorphism 
 
Anthropomorphism is a psychological tendency where people attribute human traits, emotions, and 
behaviors to non-human entities, including animals, objects, and technology. This is evident in how people 
may talk to computers, treat robotic cleaners like pets, or give names to their devices. Advertisers often 
leverage this human inclination by creating human-like or animal-like characters from inanimate objects 
to make their products more appealing. For example, breakfast cereals or fruit drinks may be personified 
with human qualities, such as talking, moving, and showing emotions, to entice consumers, especially 
children, who are more likely to accept these animated, anthropomorphic figures. 
The connection between anthropomorphism and children’s enjoyment of objects with human-like 
qualities has influenced the design of virtual agents, interactive dolls, robots, and toys. One early example 
is ActiMates, designed to motivate children to learn by interacting with them through speech and 
movement. Characters like Barney, a dinosaur, were programmed to encourage children by reacting to 
their actions, celebrating correct answers, and displaying emotions like joy or concern based on the 
content they encountered. Similarly, interactive dolls such as Mealtime Magic Mia respond to actions like 
feeding by showing emotions (e.g., blinking, smiling, or making sounds). 
Designers have found that giving technologies human-like characteristics not only enhances enjoyment 
but also encourages engagement and motivates activities, such as learning. Addressing users directly in a 
friendly, personable manner ("Hello, Rowan! Nice to see you again.") is far more inviting than impersonal, 
third-person instructions ("User 24, commence Exercise 5"), making interactions more relatable and 
reducing anxiety, especially for children. This approach is also effective in educational software, where 
characters like virtual tutors can provide a more engaging experience than a simple dialog box. 
  
Commercial physical robots have been developed to support caregiving for older adults, aiming to assist 
in social interactions and improve their experiences in care homes. Early prototypes were typically small 
(around 2 feet tall), made from white plastic with colored parts representing clothing or hair, often 
featuring large eyes and holding a tablet to display messages. These robots were designed to foster 
interaction and reduce feelings of loneliness among residents. 
An example of such a robot is Stevie, developed with a rolling base, movable arms, and a head that 
displays cartoon eyes and a mouth. Stevie was trialed in 2018–19 at a retirement community in 
Washington DC, with the goal of learning from both staff and residents to improve its functionalities. The 
robot was programmed to engage residents by leading activities like bingo and sing-alongs. Feedback 
from the trial was largely positive, with staff and residents appreciating Stevie’s ability to entertain and 
motivate. 
However, while social robots like Stevie can play a supportive role, there were also limitations noted 
regarding their capacity to entertain or engage fully. Despite their ability to assist, it was emphasized that 
these robots can never replicate the genuine human touch and emotional warmth that patients require, 
reinforcing the importance of human caregivers in the care process.
