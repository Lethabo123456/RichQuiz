8.4.3 Semi-Structured Interviews   
Semi-structured interviews blend elements of both structured and unstructured interviews, incorporating 
both closed and open questions. The interviewer follows a basic script to ensure all topics are covered, 
starting with pre-planned questions and then probing the interviewee for more information until no new 
relevant insights emerge. For example, if an interviewee mentions a specific music website they prefer, 
the interviewer might probe further by asking why they like it and for more details about the site’s layout. 

It’s important for the interviewer not to lead the interviewee with biased questions that imply a certain 
answer, such as suggesting the interviewee liked a particular aspect of the site. The interviewer’s body 
language can also influence responses, so it’s crucial to remain neutral and give the interviewee time to 
answer. Probes, like “Do you want to tell me anything else?” or prompts that remind the interviewee of 
forgotten terms, help to keep the interview on track without introducing bias. 

While semi-structured interviews are flexible and can be tailored based on the conversation, they are 
designed to be broadly replicable, with probing and prompting used to gather additional information 
without leading the interviewee. 
8.4.4 Focus Groups   
Interviews are typically one-on-one, but group interviews, such as focus groups, are also common in 
interaction design activities. Focus groups usually involve three to ten participants, guided by a trained 
facilitator, and are designed to represent a cross-section of the target population. For example, in 
evaluating an interactive university campus map, separate focus groups may be formed for 
administrators, faculty, students, and visitors, as each group uses the map differently. Focus groups are 
also used in requirements activities to identify conflicts among stakeholders' expectations.
