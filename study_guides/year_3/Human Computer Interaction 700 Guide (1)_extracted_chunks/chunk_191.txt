4.3.6 Embodied Interaction  
 
Our interactions with technology and the world can be understood as embodied, which means engaging 
practically with both the social and physical environment. This includes interacting with everyday objects 
like cups and spoons, as well as technology such as smartphones and robots. Objects and devices often 
provide clear cues on how they should be used, like a book left open on a desk to remind someone of an 
unfinished task. 
Embodied interaction involves how our bodies and active experiences shape our perceptions, emotions, 
and thoughts. Our sensorimotor experiences—such as climbing, walking, or holding objects—form the 
basis of both concrete and abstract thinking. These experiences help us develop a sense of the world and 
understand concepts like inside-outside or up-down. In human-computer interaction (HCI), embodied 
interaction is used to describe how the body mediates our interactions with technology and our emotional 
responses. 

The concept of embodied interactions has provided valuable insights for technology design. It reveals how 
physical actions influence mental processes. For instance, Barbara Tversky highlights how movement 
contributes to spatial thinking, enabling us to envision activities like assembling furniture or 
understanding crowd movement. Theorists like David Kirsh suggest that embodied interaction theories 
can inform the design of better technologies by recognizing how people think with their bodies as well as 
their minds. For example, dancers often use a practice method called "marking," where they perform 
abbreviated movements rather than a full dance routine. This allows them to focus on specific parts of 
the movement without the mental load of a complete performance. Kirsh’s research implies that learning 
complex tasks might benefit from embodied methods, such as teaching through marked actions or using 
augmented reality to simulate simplified movements instead of fully immersive simulations.
