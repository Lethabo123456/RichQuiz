Emotional interaction focuses on understanding how people feel—whether happy, sad, annoyed, 
anxious, frustrated, motivated, or ecstatic—and leveraging this knowledge to shape user experiences. 
However, designing for emotions is not straightforward. For instance, if an interface detects a user 
smiling, should it aim to sustain their happiness? Or, if it recognizes a negative emotion, like a scowl, 
should it attempt to improve their mood? 
Once an emotional state is detected, designers must decide how the system should respond. Should it 
“smile” back by incorporating expressive elements like emojis, icons, or feedback? How expressive should 
it be? The response depends on whether a particular emotional state is deemed desirable for the user 
experience or task at hand. For example, fostering a happy mood might be optimal when someone is 
shopping online, as it could make them more inclined to complete a purchase. 

Advertising agencies have long employed strategies to evoke emotional responses. They often use visuals, 
such as a picture of a cute animal or a child with large, pleading eyes, to evoke sadness or empathy. These 
tactics are designed to “pull at the heartstrings,” encouraging actions like donations or support. Similarly, 
web pages can be crafted to elicit strong emotional reactions, as shown in Figure 6.2, which highlights a

By thoughtfully integrating emotional cues, designers can create interfaces that not only respond to but 
also shape users' feelings in meaningful and purposeful ways. 
 
Our moods and emotions are dynamic and ever-changing, making it challenging to predict how we feel at 
any given moment. Emotions can arise suddenly and fade just as quickly, such as being startled by an 
unexpected loud noise. Conversely, some emotions linger for extended periods. For instance, annoyance 
might persist for hours if staying in a hotel room with a noisy air conditioner, while jealousy can simmer 
over time, resurfacing whenever triggered by a related person or situation. 
While the terms emotion, mood, and feeling are often used interchangeably, they differ in duration and 
context. Emotions are typically immediate responses to specific triggers, such as crying upon hearing sad 
news. Moods, on the other hand, represent a broader frame of mind or disposition that can develop and 
persist over longer periods, such as being in a good mood for an entire day. Feelings can encapsulate 
both; they might express an emotion (e.g., feeling sad) or reflect a mood (e.g., feeling grumpy).
