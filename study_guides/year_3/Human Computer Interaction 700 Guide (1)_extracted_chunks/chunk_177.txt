4.3 Cognitive Frameworks  
 
Several conceptual frameworks have been created to understand and predict user behaviour through 
cognitive theories. These frameworks can be categorized into two groups: those focused on mental 
processes and those that explain human interactions with technology within specific contexts. The mental 
process-based frameworks include mental models, gulfs of execution and evaluation, and information 
processing. The context-based frameworks include distributed cognition, external cognition, and 
embodied interaction. These frameworks offer valuable insights into how people think and engage with 
technology. 

4.3.1 Mental Models  
Mental models are cognitive tools people use to understand and reason about technology, especially 
when faced with unexpected issues or unfamiliar products. As individuals gain more experience with a 
product, their mental model becomes more refined. For instance, broadband engineers develop a deep 
mental model of how Wi-Fi networks function, allowing them to set up and troubleshoot networks. In 
contrast, an average person may have a basic understanding of how to use a Wi-Fi network but lacks in-
depth knowledge of its inner workings. According to cognitive psychology, mental models are internal 
representations of the external world, which people manipulate to make predictions and inferences. This 
process can involve both conscious and unconscious thinking, often involving the activation of images and 
analogies. 
 
People often use incorrect mental models due to their reliance on general assumptions about how things 
work, such as the "more is more" principle. For example, people may believe that turning a knob or 
pressing a button multiple times will increase the desired effect, as seen with faucets, but this doesn’t 

apply to thermostats, which operate on an on-off switch principle. This is because people tend to 
generalize their experiences with physical devices and apply these assumptions to different technologies, 
regardless of their functionality. 
Erroneous mental models are common and can be seen in everyday situations, such as when people press 
the pedestrian crossing button multiple times, thinking it will speed up the process. Many individuals also 
have a poor understanding of how complex technologies, like the internet or AI, work. These mental 
models are often incomplete, confusing, and based on inappropriate analogies, making it difficult for 
people to identify or solve problems. 
To help users form better mental models, interfaces can be designed to provide clear instructions, 
accessible online help, video tutorials, and context-sensitive guidance. Additionally, offering background 
information on how things work and using intuitive affordances and appropriate metaphors can improve 
user understanding.
