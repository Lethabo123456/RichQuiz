8.6 Observation   
 
Observation is a valuable method at any stage of product development. Early on, it helps designers 
understand the users' context, tasks, and goals. Later in development, it can be used to assess how well 
a prototype meets these needs. 
Observation can be conducted directly, where the investigator watches individuals perform tasks, or 
indirectly, by studying records of the activity after the fact (Bernard, 2017). It can also occur in two 
settings: in the wild or in a controlled environment. In the wild, participants are observed as they carry 
out tasks in their natural settings, whereas in a controlled environment, they perform specific tasks within 
a usability laboratory setting.

8.6.1 Direct Observation in the Wild  
 
It can be challenging for people to accurately explain their actions or describe how they complete tasks. 
Relying solely on interviews or questionnaires may not provide a complete or true understanding. 
Observation in the wild can offer valuable insights into how people behave and interact with technology, 
revealing details and nuances that may not emerge through other methods. Understanding the context 
of these activities helps explain why they happen in certain ways. 
However, observing in natural settings is often more complex than anticipated. It can result in large 
amounts of data, some of which may be tedious to analyze and less relevant. It is crucial to have a clear 
goal for the observation, especially since there’s so much happening during the process. At the same time, 
it's important to remain flexible and adjust the plan if circumstances change. For example, if an 
unexpected but relevant meeting arises, shifting the focus of the observation to include it could provide 
valuable information. Balancing a structured approach with the flexibility to adapt is a skill that improves 
with experience. 

8.7 Putting the Techniques to Work 
 
The techniques introduced here can be used individually, but it's more common to combine them for a 
single project, with potential adaptations. Combining multiple data gathering techniques into one 
program is a standard practice, especially in case study research. The advantage of this approach is that 
it provides multiple perspectives, which is crucial for achieving triangulation. Additionally, it offers a more 
comprehensive understanding by gathering data on different aspects of the activity or context under 
study.
