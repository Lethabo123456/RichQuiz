3.5.2 Conversing   
The conversing interaction type is based on a system acting as a dialogue partner, engaging in two-way 
communication with the user. Unlike the instructing interaction, where the user simply gives commands, 
conversing involves a more human-like exchange, where the system responds to the user as if it were 
another person. 

This type of interaction is most commonly used in applications where the user seeks specific information 
or wishes to discuss topics. Examples include advisory systems, help facilities, chatbots, and robots. The 
conversation can range from simple voice-recognition systems (e.g., banking inquiries or ticket booking) 
to more complex natural language-based systems that can understand and respond to queries typed in 
or spoken by the user. 

For instance, a help system could respond to a query like “How do I change the margin widths?” by 
providing detailed instructions. Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI), particularly in speech 
recognition, have led to significant improvements in conversational systems, allowing companies to use 
speech-based and chatbot-based interactions for customer queries. 
The key benefit of a conversational interaction model is that it mimics familiar human communication, 
making it intuitive and accessible. For example, Apple’s Siri enables users to interact as if speaking to 
another person, making requests such as making calls, scheduling meetings, or checking the weather in a 
natural, conversational manner. 

A challenge that arises from conversational-based interaction is when tasks become cumbersome and 
overly one-sided, especially in systems like automated phone-based systems. These systems often use 
auditory menus, which can require users to go through multiple layers of options before achieving their 
goal. This process can be tedious and frustrating. 
For example, an automated insurance company reception system might begin with a general greeting: 
"Welcome to St. Paul’s Insurance Company. Press 1 if you are a new customer; 2 if you are an 
existing customer." 
After selecting an option, the user is presented with another set of options: 
"Thank you for calling St. Paul’s Insurance Company. If you require house insurance, say 1; car 
insurance, say 2; travel insurance, say 3; health insurance, say 4; other, say 5." 
The user selects car insurance, but the interaction continues: 
"You have reached the car insurance division. If you require information about fully comprehensive 
insurance, say 1; third-party insurance, say 2..." 

This sequence becomes repetitive, and users can feel trapped in a one-sided exchange, making the 
experience less efficient and more frustrating. The goal—such as speaking to a real human or accessing 
the necessary information—becomes harder to achieve in a timely manner.
