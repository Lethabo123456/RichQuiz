When designing a robot voice interface for customer service, it's essential to ask critical questions that 
explore various aspects of the design. These questions could include considerations such as: 

• How intelligent does the robot need to be? 
• What type of movements should the robot make to appear as if it is speaking? 
• How will customers perceive the robot—will they find it engaging or gimmicky? 
• Could the robot be designed with a specific personality, like a grumpy or funny character? 
• What happens if the robot mishears an order? 
• What are the limitations of this approach? 

For example, a robot designed to serve customers could be more machine-like, as seen in some robots 
that emerged post-pandemic. These robots, which appear more like a bookshelf with trays, don’t engage 
in conversation but assist waiters by transporting food and dishes, saving time without replacing human 
staff. This robot design addresses a real-world problem—helping waiters who are hard to find post-
pandemic—while providing practical support in a way that feels more like a tool than an interactive 
experience. 

In contrast, a humanoid robot trying to converse with customers might raise concerns about its potential 
to feel gimmicky or create awkward interactions. The goal is to find a balance between functionality and 
user experience, ensuring that the design addresses real problems while being engaging and feasible. 
 
In the early stages of a design project, especially when proposing a new product, there are numerous 
uncertainties that need to be addressed. It's valuable to trace the origins of your innovative ideas, 
identifying the sources of inspiration and any theories or research that can inform and support these early 
concepts.
 
The process of questioning, challenging assumptions, and clearly articulating concerns and perspectives 
is essential during the initial ideation phase. Framing ideas as a collection of concepts is crucial for 
transforming abstract thoughts and unrealistic aspirations into practical models. This helps clarify how 
the product will function, which design features are necessary, and the level of functionality required. In 
this chapter, we explore various approaches to conceptualizing interaction to facilitate this 
transformation.
