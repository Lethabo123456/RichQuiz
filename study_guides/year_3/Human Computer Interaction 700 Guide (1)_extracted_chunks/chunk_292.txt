Chapter 8: DATA GATHERING   
 
8.1 Introduction 
 
Data is everywhere, and many people often remark that we are overwhelmed by it because of its sheer 
volume. But what exactly is data? It encompasses numbers, words, measurements, descriptions, 
comments, photos, sketches, videos, and almost anything else that helps in understanding a particular 
design, stakeholders' objectives, or human behavior. Data can be categorized as either quantitative or 
qualitative. For instance, the time someone takes to locate information on a webpage and the number of 
clicks required are examples of quantitative data. On the other hand, a person’s feedback about the 
webpage constitutes qualitative data. 

This raises questions about how such data is collected, what techniques are available, and how reliable 
and valuable the collected data is. This chapter explores commonly used methods for data collection in 
interaction design activities. Data gathering plays a key role in both identifying requirements and 
conducting evaluations. During the requirements phase, it helps gather enough information to advance 
the design process. In the evaluation phase, it captures participants’ feedback and performance with a 
system or prototype. The techniques discussed in this chapter require minimal to no programming or 
technical expertise. Advanced methods for managing large datasets, such as extracting data from online 

platforms like Twitter, along with the ethical implications of such practices, are covered in Chapter 10, 
"Data at Scale and Ethical Concerns." 
The chapter introduces three primary data-gathering techniques: interviews, questionnaires, and 
observation. The subsequent chapter will focus on analyzing and interpreting the collected data. 
Interviews involve an interviewer asking one or more participants a series of questions, which can be 
structured or unstructured. These interviews are typically conducted synchronously, often face-to-face, 
but they can also be asynchronous, such as through email or chat, and are frequently carried out remotely. 
Questionnaires, in contrast, consist of a set of questions designed to be completed asynchronously 
without the investigator's presence, and they can be distributed online or in paper format. Observations 
can be direct, where participants are watched as they perform activities in real time, or indirect, where 
their activities are recorded for later analysis. All three methods can be employed to collect both 
qualitative and quantitative data. 
While these techniques are foundational and relatively simple, they are versatile and can be combined or 
adapted in various ways. It is essential not to rely solely on one method but to use a combination to 
minimize biases inherent in any single approach.
