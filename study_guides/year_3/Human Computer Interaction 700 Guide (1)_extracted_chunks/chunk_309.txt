8.3 Capturing Data 
Certain data collection methods, like questionnaires, diaries, interaction logging, web scraping, and 
gathering work artifacts, are self-documenting, meaning no additional recording is needed. However, 
other methods allow for a range of recording options, including note-taking, photography, audio 
recording, and video recording. Often, multiple recording techniques are used in combination. For 
example, an interview may be audio recorded, and a photograph of the interviewee or their environment 
could be taken to provide context for later analysis. 
The choice of recording method depends on factors such as the study's objectives, the intended use of 
the data, the context, available time and resources, and the sensitivity of the situation. The selected 
approach will influence the level of detail captured and the intrusiveness of the data collection. In many 
cases, audio recordings, photos, and notes are sufficient, but in some situations, video recording is 
essential to capture both the activity and its context. 
With modern recording devices being lightweight and affordable, and digital technologies integrated into 
daily life, capturing data has become easier. However, it's crucial to focus on collecting only relevant data, 
which requires careful planning. Additionally, informed consent must be obtained, and precautions 
should be taken to avoid unintentionally capturing individuals in the background or including irrelevant 
details in photos or recordings. This is particularly challenging in settings like in-the-wild studies 
conducted in people's homes. Beyond the ethical considerations of data capture and storage, collecting 
more data than necessary can lead to time-consuming and error-prone sorting. The following sections will 
explore three common data recording methods. 

8.3.1 Notes Plus Photographs   
Note-taking, whether by hand or typing, is a simple yet highly adaptable method of data collection, even 
though it may seem outdated. Handwritten notes can later be transcribed, either in part or fully, which, 
although time-consuming, is often the first step in data analysis and provides the researcher with an 
overview of the data’s quality and content. While there are digital tools to assist with data collection and 
analysis, handwritten notes offer key advantages, such as being less intrusive than typing and allowing for 
more flexibility—such as sketching diagrams of workspaces. Additionally, many researchers find that 
writing notes helps them focus on what matters most and encourages early reflections on the data. 
However, note-taking also has its drawbacks. It can be challenging to capture key points accurately, and 
it can be tiring to write while listening or observing. It’s easy to lose focus, and biases may emerge. Also, 
handwritten notes can sometimes be hard to read, and the speed of writing may limit how much 
information is captured. Collaborating with a colleague can help mitigate some of these challenges and 
provide a fresh perspective. 
To complement notes and sketches, photographs, screenshots, and short videos of artifacts, events, or 
environments can be valuable additions to the data collection process.
