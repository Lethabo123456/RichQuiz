4.2.1 Attention   
Attention is crucial in daily life as it allows us to stay aware of our surroundings and multitask. It helps us 
focus on important tasks, such as crossing the road safely, responding to someone calling our name, or 
texting while watching TV. Attention involves choosing what to focus on from various options, based on 
the current situation. The ease or difficulty of this process depends on two factors: having clear goals and 
the prominence of the relevant information in the environment. 

Clear Goals 
Clear goals help individuals focus their attention on relevant information. For example, someone who has 
just arrived at an airport after a flight without Wi-Fi may look for news on the World Cup by scanning 
headlines on their phone or a public TV display. When unsure about what they're seeking, people might 
browse information more freely, letting it guide their attention. For instance, when at a restaurant, 
someone may have the general goal of eating but a vague idea of what to order. They scan the menu, 
letting descriptions and factors like cost, company, and specials influence their decision.

Multitasking and Attention   
As highlighted in the chapter introduction, many people now engage in multitasking, frequently switching 
attention between various tasks. Extensive research has examined the advantages and drawbacks of 
multitasking across different groups, with the highest prevalence seen among young people. For instance, 
Karen Ettinger and Anat Cohen (2020) found that teens often multitask by combining activities such as 
listening to music, texting, and social networking. 

A common question is whether multitasking can be performed without negatively affecting the tasks 
involved. Studies have focused on how multitasking impacts memory and attention (Burgess, 2015), 
revealing that the effects depend on the nature of the tasks and the level of attention each requires. For 
example, listening to soft music while working can help individuals block out distractions like traffic or 
conversations, aiding focus. However, loud music can be disruptive. 

There are also differences between light and heavy multitaskers. Heavy multitaskers frequently switch 
between tasks using various media channels, while light multitaskers focus on one task at a time, only 
occasionally switching tasks, such as checking their phone. Studies comparing the two types have shown 
that heavy multitaskers are more prone to distraction from multiple media sources, whereas light

multitaskers are better at managing competing distractions (Ophir et al., 2009). This suggests that heavy 
multitaskers may struggle to filter out irrelevant information and are more easily distracted, though it 
remains unclear if being easily distracted leads to heavy multitasking. 
Multitasking is considered detrimental to performance because it overloads the ability to focus. Switching 
attention between tasks requires additional effort, and it can take time to regain focus and recall where 
one left off. This results in longer task completion times. For example, students engaged in instant 
messaging while reading a textbook took up to 50% longer to finish the material compared to those who 
did not multitask (Bowman et al., 2010). Multitasking can also cause people to lose their train of thought, 
make mistakes, and start over. 

Recent research, however, has provided less clear conclusions. A meta-review indicated that while many 
studies show poorer performance in heavy multitaskers, some studies have not found a significant link 
between multitasking and performance (Uncapher and Wagner, 2018). Danielle Lottridge et al. (2015) 
proposed that the relationship may be more nuanced. Their study showed that while heavy multitaskers 
are easily distracted, they can use these distractions effectively if they are relevant to the task. In their 
experiment, heavy multitaskers performed just as well writing an essay when exposed to relevant 
information as they did without distractions. However, when exposed to irrelevant information, their 
performance was negatively affected. In conclusion, multitasking can have both positive and negative 
effects depending on the relevance of the distractions to the task.
