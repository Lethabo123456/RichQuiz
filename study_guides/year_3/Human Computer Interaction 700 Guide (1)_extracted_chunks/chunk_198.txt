5.2 Being Social 
 
Being social is a core aspect of daily life, involving constant interaction and updates on various events and 
activities. Friends and families share news about work, school, or leisure, while colleagues exchange 
updates on projects, deadlines, and workplace events. Though face-to-face communication remains vital, 
social media and digital tools have significantly transformed how people connect, with platforms like 
WhatsApp, Slack, and Teams now integral to personal and professional interactions. 
The rise of social media has made online communication ubiquitous, with adults maintaining extensive 
networks, often far surpassing those formed through in-person interactions. Shared calendars, 
collaboration tools like Google Docs, and social platforms such as LinkedIn have reshaped how people 
manage relationships and networks. The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated these shifts, as remote work, 
learning, and socializing became necessities. Videoconferencing tools like Zoom and Teams became 
crucial, enabling people to adapt quickly to new norms, albeit with challenges like makeshift home offices 
and digital fatigue. 

Post-pandemic, hybrid work models emerged, blending physical and remote participation. However, 
these setups often fall short of replicating the richness of in-person interactions, with issues like poor 
audio-visual quality and technology limitations. Distributed teams across global locations face added 
challenges, including time zone differences and cultural barriers. Despite ongoing improvements in 
collaborative technology, achieving the depth of face-to-face engagement remains a work in progress. 

A growing societal concern is the increasing time people spend on their phones—whether interacting with 
others, playing games, or engaging on social media—and its impact on well-being (Ali et al., 2018). A 2021 
U.S. survey revealed that over half of respondents spent 5–6 hours daily on their phones, excluding work-
related use (Ceci, 2022). Similar trends were noted in countries like Brazil, Indonesia, and South Korea 
(Wakefield, 2022). Even in social settings, people often retreat into their digital bubbles. 
In Reclaiming Conversation (2015), Sherry Turkle highlights how this trend negatively affects daily life, 
particularly face-to-face conversation. She notes that many people prefer texting over talking, as it is 
easier and more convenient. Her research indicates that when adults talk less, children follow suit, 
reducing opportunities to develop empathy. While online communication has its role, Turkle argues for 
reclaiming the art of conversation, encouraging people to put down their phones and rediscover the

The widespread adoption of social media and social computing tools has also reshaped how people 
connect and interact. Established norms and etiquette in face-to-face interactions have adapted to digital 
contexts. For example, during the pandemic, videoconferencing introduced new social rules, such as 
muting oneself when not speaking and using virtual hand-raising or emoji reactions for feedback. 
Planning social activities has also evolved. Instead of calls, people now often use texts or WhatsApp 
groups. However, this can lead to challenges like missed messages, delayed responses, and excessive 
back-and-forth communication. Additionally, last-minute decision-making, especially among teenagers, 
has become common as they wait for potentially better plans. This shift can be frustrating for planners 
trying to finalize arrangements, such as booking tickets before they sell out.
