Since then, videoconferencing has greatly evolved and become a widely adopted communication tool 
across the globe. Software development allowed these platforms to run on PCs, smartphones, and tablets, 
with many people’s first experience being Skype, followed by other free apps like FaceTime and 
WhatsApp. During the pandemic, tools like Zoom and Teams gained significant popularity. Initially used 
for basic online chats, these platforms quickly transformed into virtual meeting rooms, adding features to 
support a wider range of online activities. These included options for changing background screens, 
parallel chat windows, breakout rooms for smaller discussions, and in-the-moment emojis for expression. 
Additional popular features included screen sharing, file exchange, and digital whiteboards. To indicate 
who was speaking, effects such as enlarging the speaker’s video feed or highlighting their portal in a 
different colour became common. 

In many ways, modern videoconferencing software has greatly enhanced how we communicate, making 
it easier to switch between conversation and collaboration. Adapting to this shift was relatively 
straightforward for most people. However, while many found it to be an efficient mode of interaction, it 
also led to exhaustion, giving rise to the term "Zoom fatigue" (Bailenson, 2021). This fatigue was caused 
by factors like prolonged close-up eye contact, high cognitive demands, constant self-evaluation from 
seeing oneself on video, and the physical strain of staying in one location for hours. 
 
Much research has been dedicated to determining the best way to represent online social interaction in 
virtual spaces. A key concept that has influenced this area is social translucence (Erickson and Kellogg, 
2000), which emphasizes the need for communication systems to make both participants and their 
actions visible to one another. This principle was central to the development of Babble, an early 
communication tool created by David Smith at IBM (Erickson et al., 1999). Babble provided a dynamic 
visualization of participants in an ongoing chat room using a large 2D circle on each user’s screen. Colored 
marbles represented users: those inside the circle were active in the conversation, while marbles outside 
the circle indicated users engaged in other discussions. The more active a participant, the closer their 
marble moved toward the center; conversely, the less engaged, the further it moved toward the edge. 
Modern virtual spaces, such as Sococo and Gather, continue this idea of social translucence by displaying 
avatar icons to show who is online and in which space. In the realm of videoconferencing, 360-degree 
cameras have begun to capture panoramic views of meeting rooms. Unlike traditional webcams, which 

are fixed in one direction, 360 cameras are placed centrally on a table, offering remote team members a 
full view of everyone present. Some systems, like Meeting Owl, can even detect and zoom in on the person
