The most effective conceptual models are often those that seem simple and obvious, where the 
operations are intuitive. However, sometimes applications become overly complex due to successive 
upgrades, adding new features that complicate the original model. While tech companies often provide 
videos to explain new features, users may not pay attention and prefer to stick with the methods they are 
familiar with, which can lead to frustration when changes are made. For instance, when Facebook 
introduced a revised newsfeed, many users expressed dissatisfaction, preferring the old interface. 
Once a conceptual model is agreed upon, it serves as a shared blueprint that leads to a testable proof of 
concept. It can be represented as a textual description or a diagram, depending on the design team's 
preferred communication method. The conceptual model not only helps user experience designers but 
also communicates ideas to business, engineering, finance, product, and marketing teams. It becomes the 
foundation for developing more detailed aspects of the design, allowing for simpler designs that align 
with users' tasks, quicker development, better customer adoption, and reduced training and support 
needs. 
  
Many products are built on well-established conceptual models. For instance, the conceptual model used 
in most online shopping websites is based on the core elements of the customer experience in a store. 
This includes adding items to a shopping cart or basket and proceeding to checkout when ready to 
purchase. Designers now have access to collections of patterns that guide the design of interfaces for 
these core transactional processes, as well as other aspects of user experience. This means interaction 
designers don’t have to reinvent the wheel each time they design or redesign an application. Examples of 
these patterns include those for online forms and mobile phone navigation. 

Completely new conceptual models that revolutionize daily and work activities at an interface are rare. 
However, there have been some groundbreaking innovations, such as the desktop (developed by Xerox 
in the late 1970s), the digital spreadsheet (created by Dan Bricklin and Bob Frankston in the late 1970s), 
and the World Wide Web (created by Tim Berners-Lee in the late 1980s). These innovations made tasks 
that were once restricted to skilled individuals accessible to the broader public, significantly expanding 
what was possible. The graphical desktop, for example, transformed office work by simplifying tasks like 
creating, editing, and printing documents. Prior to this, using computers involved mastering complex 
command languages (e.g., DOS or UNIX). Digital spreadsheets made accounting tasks more flexible and 
user-friendly, enabling complex computations through interactive fields. The World Wide Web 
revolutionized access to a network of information, allowing anyone to browse it remotely. 
Since then, technologies like e-readers and digital authoring tools have introduced new ways to read 
documents and books online, offering features like annotation, highlighting, linking, commenting, 
copying, and tracking. The web has also simplified many other activities, such as browsing for news, 
weather, sports, and financial updates, as well as banking, shopping, booking tickets, and online learning. Notably, all of 
these conceptual models were based on activities that were already familiar to users.
