9.3 Design Patterns  
 
Design patterns encapsulate design knowledge, offering a unique structure and philosophy compared to 
other methods or forms of guidance. A primary goal of the patterns community is to establish a shared 
vocabulary—anchored by the names of the patterns—that enables designers to communicate effectively 
with one another and with stakeholders. Another aim is to document design expertise in a compelling 
and accessible way. 
The concept of patterns originated from architect Christopher Alexander, who introduced patterns in 
architecture (Alexander, 1979). His vision was to capture the "quality without a name"—a sense of 
inherent goodness that is instantly recognizable. In design, a pattern is essentially a solution to a recurring 
problem within a specific context. Each pattern describes the problem, the solution, and the 
circumstances in which the solution has been successful, along with the rationale for its effectiveness. 
A defining feature of design patterns is their generative nature—they can be implemented in various 
ways, making them highly adaptable. Since the late 1990s, design patterns have gained traction in 
interaction design (e.g., Borchers, 2001; Crumlish & Malone, 2009) and have continued to evolve (e.g., 
Tidwell et al., 2020; Zaina et al., 2022). 

Pattern libraries, collections, and galleries are widely used in interaction design, often focusing on user 
interface elements like icons, menus, and common functionalities. Designers favor patterns because they 
offer proven solutions to familiar challenges. Many pattern repositories also provide accompanying code 
snippets, often hosted on open-source platforms like GitHub. These snippets can frequently be integrated 
with minimal modification, saving time and effort. 

Because patterns represent familiar solutions, they enhance the user experience of new products by 
leveraging designs that users already recognize and understand. For example, content delivery patterns 
discussed in Box 13.3 demonstrate how well-established and widely adopted solutions continue to evolve 
over time, underscoring their lasting value in design practice. 
 
Patterns are valuable on their own, but their true potential is realized when they form part of a pattern 
language. A pattern language is a network of interconnected patterns that work together to create a 
comprehensive structure. While the term "pattern language" is not widely used in interaction design, the 
concepts of design systems or design languages serve a similar purpose, particularly within large 
corporations such as Airbnb, Salesforce, and Uber. 
A design system is a curated collection of core elements, reusable components, and guidelines that define 
the visual and interactive design of a product or product family. Essentially, it is a structured collection of 
patterns and components accompanied by guidelines to ensure a cohesive and consistent user 
experience. Design systems often include additional resources such as brand and accessibility guidelines. 
Beyond consistency across products, design systems offer several benefits. They reduce design and 
development effort, foster learning among designers, and enhance cross-functional collaboration 
(Churchill, 2019). Unlike isolated user interface patterns, design systems or languages facilitate the reuse 
of larger design elements, making them more impactful. 
Design systems are often supported by frameworks—collections of reusable software components that 
enable rapid implementation. Additionally, other reusable design tools, such as user flows, are 
increasingly common. For instance, platforms like Overflow.io allow designers to create playable user flow 

diagrams, while uxarchive.com provides a vast repository of reusable user flows, further streamlining the 
design process.
