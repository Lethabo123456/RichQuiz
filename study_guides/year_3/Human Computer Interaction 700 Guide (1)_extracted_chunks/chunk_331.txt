8.4.7 Enriching the Interview Experience 
 
Interviews, whether in-person or remote, can be enhanced by incorporating relevant artifacts to guide 
the discussion. These props help provide context for both the interviewer and interviewees, grounding 
the conversation in concrete examples. Examples of such artifacts include personas, prototypes, or 
scenarios (which are further explored in Chapters 11, “Discovering Requirements,” and 12, “Design, 
Prototyping, and Construction”). Figure 8.4 demonstrates how personas can be used effectively in a focus 
group setting. 
 
Almohannad Albastaki et al. (2020) explored the use of a virtual experience prototype to enhance remote 
interviews. They conducted a study with design experts using a non-immersive virtual reality simulation, 
where participants interacted with a robotic expression in a nighttime urban alley. The robot featured 64 
individual lighting components to display expressions. Afterward, participants were interviewed for 7-10 
minutes about their experience. The researchers learned that virtual prototypes could offer ecological 
validity, meaning they could yield results comparable to those from real robots for certain research 
questions. Additionally, using videoconferencing to record participants' activities minimized the need for 
extra equipment.

8.5 Questionnaires   
 
Questionnaires are a widely used method for gathering demographic data and opinions. Like interviews, 
they can include both closed and open-ended questions. Once a questionnaire is created, it can be 
distributed to many participants with minimal resources, allowing for the collection of a larger volume of 
data compared to interviews. 
The decision between using a questionnaire or a structured interview depends largely on the motivation 
of the respondents. If participants are sufficiently motivated to fill out a questionnaire without prompting, 
this method is more efficient. However, if respondents need encouragement to participate, a structured 

interview may yield better results. For instance, structured interviews are more effective in environments 
where people are on the move, such as train stations or while walking to meetings. 
Another advantage of structured interviews is that the interviewer can select participants who meet 
specific criteria. However, interviews require more ongoing resources from the researcher. Additionally, 
crafting effective questionnaire questions can be more challenging than developing structured interview 
questions, as the researcher isn't present to clarify any ambiguities.
