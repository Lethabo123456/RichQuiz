4.2.5 Reading, Speaking, and Listening 
 
Reading, speaking, and listening are three modes of language processing that share some similarities 
and differences. While the meaning of sentences remains the same across these modes, the ease with 
which people engage with each one varies depending on the individual, task, and context. For instance, 
many people find listening easier than reading. 
Key differences between the modes include:
 
• Written language is permanent, allowing for re-reading if needed, while listening is transient 
unless recorded. 
• Reading can be faster than speaking or listening, as text can be scanned quickly, unlike the linear 
nature of spoken words. 
• Listening requires less cognitive effort than reading or speaking, which is why many prefer 
audiobooks or podcasts. 
• Written language tends to be more grammatical, whereas spoken language is often informal 
and ungrammatical, with frequent interruptions or incomplete sentences. 
• People with dyslexia struggle with written language, making reading and spelling challenging. 

Various applications have been developed to support or replace these modes for people with 
difficulties: 
• Interactive books and language apps help with reading and language learning. 
• Speech recognition systems enable interaction via voice commands, such as those in 
smartphones or smart speakers. 
• Text-to-speech systems assist those with visual impairments by converting written text to 
spoken words. 
• Natural-language interfaces (e.g., chatbots) allow users to type questions and receive written 
answers. 
• Interactive apps help people with reading, writing, or speaking challenges by reading text aloud 
and proofreading. 
• Haptic feedback systems assist visually impaired individuals in playing video games, such as 
Microsoft's haptic controllers.
