It may not always be necessary or feasible to involve all stakeholders directly in the design process, but 
creating a list of stakeholders is useful for determining who should be involved and to what extent. 
Primary candidates for close involvement are typically potential users and those who will be affected by 
the product’s launch. While identifying these stakeholders may seem simple, it can often be more 
complex than anticipated. For instance, Sha Zhao and colleagues (2016) discovered that smartphone users 
are more diverse than most manufacturers realize. Their analysis of a month’s worth of smartphone app 
usage identified 382 distinct types of users, including categories like Screen Checkers and Young Parents.
For some products, such as a system for scheduling work shifts, the user community is more defined, with 

specific roles (like shop assistants) within particular industries (like retail). In this case, while there are 
various users with different roles interacting with the product in different ways, ist of stakeholders is 
more limited. Beyond direct users (such as shop assistants and schedulers), indirect users may include 
those who manage them, those who receive system outputs, and those who test the system. Those 
affected by the product might include the shop's customers, who expect the right level of service, and the 
staff’s family members. 

Once the list of stakeholders is identified and consulted, engaging the right groups can still be challenging. 
Katie Seaborn and colleagues (2020) highlight several difficulties in involving the user community in a 
large urban food waste recycling project, even with partnerships from various stakeholders. After trying 
different methods to engage residents, such as word of mouth, community meetings, cold calling, and 
posting flyers, the researchers took a unique approach: they observed the contents of existing bins to gain 
insights into waste habits.
