8.2.2 Identifying Participants 
 
The goals established for a data-gathering session help define the types of individuals from whom data 
will be collected, referred to as the population or study population. In some instances, this group may be 
small and easy to identify, such as when there are few stakeholders with accessible contact. However, it 
is often necessary to select participants from a larger pool, a process known as sampling. When the entire 
population is accessible, it is called saturation sampling, though this is rare. Typically, only a subset of the 
population participates, using either probability or nonprobability sampling methods. 
Probability sampling methods include simple random sampling, where participants are chosen using a 
random number generator or by selecting every nth person on a list, and stratified sampling, which 
involves dividing the population into groups (e.g., school classes) before applying random sampling. 
Nonprobability sampling methods, such as convenience sampling and volunteer panels, rely on 
participants' availability or willingness to participate. In convenience sampling, the sample comprises 

those who are readily available rather than specifically selected. A variation of this is snowball sampling, 
where current participants recruit additional participants, creating a chain effect. 
The key distinction between probability and nonprobability sampling lies in generalizability. Probability 
sampling allows for statistical analysis and generalization to the broader population, while nonprobability 
sampling does not offer robust generalizations. Statistical methods also require a sufficient number of 
participants. Vera Toepoel (2016) provides an in-depth discussion of sampling, particularly for survey 
research. 
Crowdsourcing platforms, such as Prolific, provide access to a large and potentially diverse participant 
pool, effectively supporting a range of studies. These platforms allow for detailed screening based on 
various characteristics, including demographics, shopping habits, work situations, hobbies, political or 
religious beliefs, and even handedness. Specifying participant profiles remains crucial to ensure relevant 
and meaningful data collection. Further discussions on the use of crowdsourcing and evaluation can be 
found in Chapters 10 and 14.
