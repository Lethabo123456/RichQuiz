4.2.2 Perception  
 
Perception is the process of acquiring information from the environment through the five senses—vision, 
hearing, taste, smell, and touch—and transforming this data into experiences of objects, events, sounds, 
and tastes (Roth, 1986). Additionally, the sense of kinesthesia, which relates to the awareness of body 
movement and position, involves internal sensory organs called proprioceptors in the muscles and joints. 
Perception is complex and involves other cognitive processes such as memory, attention, and language. 
Vision is the most dominant sense for sighted individuals, followed by hearing and touch. In interaction 
design, it’s crucial to present information in a way that ensures it is perceived as intended. 
As shown in Activity 4.1, grouping items together and leaving spaces between them can enhance 
perception by breaking up information. This approach makes it easier to scan content, compared to a 
continuous block of text. Many designers also recommend using white space when grouping elements, as 
it helps users perceive and locate items more efficiently (WordPress, 2022).

4.2.3 Memory   
 
Memory allows individuals to recall various types of knowledge to act appropriately, such as recognizing 
faces, remembering names, and recalling past interactions. However, it is impossible to remember 
everything we encounter, and our brains use a filtering process to decide which information is worth 
further processing and memorizing. This filtering process is not perfect, as we often forget things we wish 
to remember and remember things we would rather forget. For example, we may struggle to recall 
people's names or scientific facts, while trivia or catchy tunes may linger in our minds effortlessly. 
Memory can be categorized into working memory, which refers to recalling recent information, such as 
the last thing someone said, and long-term memory, which involves recalling distant memories, like a 
tune from decades ago. The filtering process in both working and long-term memory starts with encoding, 
which determines what information gets attention and how it is interpreted. The more attention and 
cognitive processing information receives, the more likely it is to be remembered. For instance, reflecting 
on a topic, engaging in exercises, discussing it with others, and taking notes are more effective for 
retention than passive activities like reading or watching a video. 
The context in which information is encoded also affects its retrieval. People may struggle to recall 
information that was learned in a different context than the one they are currently in.
