1.9.3 Design Principles   
 
Design principles are guidelines that help interaction designers focus on various aspects of the user 
experience. These principles serve as general abstractions that guide designers in creating more intuitive 
and effective interfaces. Examples of such principles include: 
• Feedback: It ensures users know the outcome of their actions, helping them understand what to 
do next in an interface. For example, when using a device, feedback may come in the form of 
visual, auditory, or tactile responses. 
• Findability and Navigability: These principles ensure users can easily locate objects or functions, 
like navigating a website or finding an option on a device. Clear and structured menus and 
navigation paths are key here. 
• Constraints: These are restrictions placed on user interactions to prevent mistakes. For example, 
certain menu options may be disabled at specific stages of a task. 
• Consistency: This principle focuses on using uniform operations and elements across the interface 
to simplify the learning process. Consistent design allows users to predict and understand 
interactions more easily. 
• Affordance: Affordance refers to how an object or interface element indicates how it should be 
used. For example, a button should look like it can be pressed. There are two types of affordance: 
real affordances (physical objects like a door handle) and perceived affordances (interface 
elements like buttons or links, which users learn to interact with through experience). 
These principles are derived from a combination of theory, experience, and common sense and act as 
triggers for designers to consider various aspects of user interaction. While they don't provide exact 
solutions, they guide designers in ensuring usability and satisfaction in their designs.
