6.3 Expressive Interfaces: Aesthetic or Annoying?  

A variety of features have been introduced to enhance the expressiveness of interfaces, such as emojis, 
sounds, colors, shapes, icons, animations, videos, photos, and virtual agents. In addition to visual 
elements, expressivity can also be conveyed through "sonifications," which highlight actions and events—
like a whoosh sound for a window closing, a schlook for a file being dragged, or a ding for a new email—
and through vibrotactile feedback, such as unique smartphone vibrations to signal specific messages from 
friends or family. The main goal is typically to either (1) establish an emotional connection or evoke 
feelings like warmth or sadness, or (2) provoke specific emotional responses, such as comfort, ease, or 
happiness.
 
Many websites, e-commerce platforms, and applications effectively utilize a mix of these expressive 
features. Retail websites like Nike and Levis, for instance, have been creating visually rich online shopping 
experiences for years, incorporating high-quality videos, emotive music, and impactful imagery on their 
landing pages (see Figure 6.6). These sites are particularly captivating and engaging, especially for their 
target audience, and they successfully elicit emotional responses. 
 
Expressive features, however, can sometimes be more irritating than aesthetically pleasing. A well-known 
example is Clippy, Microsoft’s paperclip assistant designed to appear friendly by displaying human-like 
traits. It would show up at the bottom of the screen whenever the system assumed a user needed help 
with a task (see Figure 6.7a), using googly eyes and eyebrows to convey expressiveness. Initially, it was 
amusing and seemed helpful, but after repeated appearances, many users began to find it intrusive and 
distracting. 

Its most common intervention would be to say, “It looks like you’re writing a letter” and offer 
assistance. This might be helpful the first time someone was writing a letter, but became annoying after 
repeated prompts. Some even found Clippy offensive. Media coverage has explored the reasons for its 
failure, including being ahead of its time and having a poorly designed interface. For example, The New 
Yorker (2015) reported that in a focus group discussing why Clippy was disliked, some women remarked 
that the character seemed too masculine. 

Since then, other virtual agents have been created to assist users at interfaces, often represented as 
avatars (like Anna in Figure 6.7b, now defunct) with limited expressions, such as eyebrow movements and 
blinking eyes. These avatars are designed to be friendly and helpful, guiding users to the information they 
need, but they can also become annoying or intrusive, especially when users already know what they 
want. In these situations, they can come across as pushy salespeople. 
The challenge is how to design virtual agents that are helpful and friendly without being irritating. For 
starters, they should appear only occasionally at the interface. They should also have a pleasant demeanor 
but avoid being overly human-like or too personable. Another question is the gender representation of 
these agents. Many have been designed as female, but this may reinforce gender stereotypes. A gender-
neutral cartoon character, such as an animal or robot, could be a better option. 

The impact of aesthetically pleasing interfaces on usability has also been the subject of research. Noam 
Tractinsky (2013) has consistently demonstrated how the aesthetics of an interface can enhance users' 
perceptions of its usability. When an interface is visually appealing and enjoyable—such as through 
beautiful graphics, a pleasant tactile experience, or well-organized elements—users are more likely to be 
patient and willing to wait a few extra seconds for a website to load. Additionally, visually attractive 
interfaces tend to be more satisfying and enjoyable to interact with.
