8.4 Interviews  

Interviews are often described as a "conversation with a purpose" (Kahn and Cannell, 1957). The level of 
similarity to a typical conversation depends on the interview type, which falls into four categories: open-
ended (unstructured), structured, semi-structured, and group interviews. The first three types are 
distinguished by the degree of control the interviewer has, based on whether they follow a predetermined 
set of questions. The fourth type, known as a focus group, involves a small group guided by a facilitator, 
which may range from informal to structured facilitation. 

The choice of interview approach depends on the interview's purpose, the questions being addressed, 
and the interaction design activity. For example, if the goal is to explore people’s impressions of a new 
design concept, an informal, open-ended interview is often most effective. However, if the aim is to gather 
feedback on a specific design feature, such as the layout of a web browser, a structured interview or 
questionnaire is typically preferred due to its more focused questions. In some cases, interviewees may 
be asked to bring items, such as documents or photographs, to help illustrate specific points. 

8.4.1 Unstructured Interviews   
Open-ended or unstructured interviews are on one end of the spectrum in terms of how much control 
the interviewer has over the process. These interviews are exploratory and resemble conversations, often 
allowing for deep exploration of topics. The questions posed are open-ended, meaning there is no specific 
expectation for the format or content of the answers. For example, an initial question could be: “What 
are the advantages and disadvantages of using a wearable?” The interviewee has the freedom to answer 
in any way they choose, and both the interviewer and the interviewee can guide the conversation. The 
interviewer might ask follow-up questions like, “Can you tell me more about that?” which is known as 
probing. 

Although unstructured interviews are open-ended, the interviewer should have a plan outlining the key 
topics to ensure that all relevant areas are covered. It’s important to differentiate between being open to 
new ideas and having no plan at all. Conducting an unstructured interview requires balancing the need to 
get answers to specific questions while being prepared to explore unexpected areas of discussion. 
One advantage of unstructured interviews is that they generate rich, complex data that provides a deep 
understanding of the topic. These interviews may also uncover issues the interviewer had not initially 
considered. However, because the format is flexible, the data from each interview will vary, and analyzing 
the data can be time-consuming. Despite this, unstructured interviews can provide valuable insights, with 
themes emerging across interviews through techniques like grounded theory, as discussed in data analysis 
methods.
