4.2 What Is Cognition?  

Cognition encompasses various mental activities, including thinking, remembering, learning, 
daydreaming, decision-making, seeing, reading, writing, and speaking. A traditional way to categorize 
these cognitive processes is by distinguishing between experiential and reflective modes (Norman, 1993). 
Experiential cognition refers to automatic, intuitive, and effortless mental states where individuals 
respond to their environment without conscious thought, often honed through experience and expertise. 
Examples include driving, reading, having a conversation, or watching a video. On the other hand, 
reflective cognition involves more deliberate mental effort, requiring attention, judgment, and decision-
making. It is typically associated with activities like designing, learning, and writing reports, where new 
ideas and creativity may emerge. Both types of cognition are essential for everyday functioning. 

Another common framework for describing cognition is through fast and slow thinking (Kahneman, 
2011). Fast thinking aligns with experiential cognition, being instinctive, reflexive, and effortless. It 
operates without much voluntary control, like quickly solving simple arithmetic, such as 2 + 2. In contrast, 
slow thinking requires more time and is more logical and demanding, often involving tasks like multiplying 
21 × 19, which requires greater concentration and effort. People often resort to fast thinking today, using 
tools like calculators to simplify such tasks. 

In interaction design, cognition is further explored in terms of context, tools, interfaces, and the people 
involved (Rogers, 2012). Cognition can be distributed, situated, extended, or embodied, depending on 
when, where, and how it occurs. Furthermore, cognition is understood as a set of specific processes 
(Eysenck and Brysbaert, 2018), including: 
 
• Attention 
• Perception 
• Memory 
• Learning 
• Reading, speaking, and listening 
• Problem-solving, planning, reasoning, and decision-making 

Many of these cognitive processes are interrelated, and multiple processes can be engaged in a single 
activity. For example, when reading, we must pay attention to the text, recognize the letters and words, 
and make sense of the overall meaning. 
In the subsequent sections, we will explore these cognitive processes in greater detail, focusing on how 
they influence interaction design, particularly attention and memory, which are the most relevant to 
user experience. We will also highlight key design implications for each cognitive process.
