Alan Cooper, a recognized user experience expert, laments that many modern software products still 
suffer from interaction issues that have persisted for decades. Despite over 30 years of interaction design 
knowledge, many products still fail to adhere to basic usability principles, such as providing an "undo" 
option. To improve user experiences, we need to focus on reducing frustrations while enhancing 
enjoyment and effectiveness. This requires a deeper understanding of interaction design, aiming to create 
products that are not only functional but also easy to use and pleasurable. This chapter will explore these 
concepts, helping you understand the basics of interaction design and the process of evaluating product 
usability. 
 
1.2 Good and Poor Design  
 
A key focus of interaction design is creating interactive products that are easy to learn, effective to use, 
and provide a satisfying experience for the intended users. One way to understand what makes a product 
usable is by comparing well-designed products with poorly designed ones. Identifying the strengths and 
weaknesses of different interactive products can help clarify what usability truly means. For example, the 
common remote control serves as a useful case study, with many versions over the years suffering from 
poor design. These remote controls often have a confusing array of small, multi-colored buttons, making it 
difficult for users to complete even simple tasks, such as pausing the TV or accessing the main menu. 
In contrast, the design of the TiVo remote control demonstrates how thoughtful interaction design can 
lead to a much more user-friendly experience. The TiVo remote was specifically created with the viewer 
in mind, featuring large, clearly labeled buttons that are logically arranged for easy navigation. Its 
ergonomic, peanut-shaped form fits comfortably in the hand, and the playful, colorful design with cartoon
icons makes the buttons easy to identify. This intentional design prioritizes usability by making the remote 
not only functional but also enjoyable to use. By comparing these two examples, we can see the 
importance of considering the user's experience in the design process to ensure a product is both usable 
and engaging.
