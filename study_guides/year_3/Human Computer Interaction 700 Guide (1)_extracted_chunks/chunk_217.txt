5.4.2 Telepresence  
  
Telepresence offers another method for creating virtual spaces, where individuals perceive themselves 
as being present in a physical location with others, despite being in a different location (e.g., at home). 
Telepresence robots are specifically designed for this purpose, allowing people to attend events and 
communicate remotely by controlling the robots. Unlike traditional video calls, where individuals are 
confined to a fixed camera view, telepresence robots enable users to move the robot around and control 
its camera to explore the environment, fostering a greater sense of connection with the place and people 
involved. 
These robots have also been used to help individuals with developmental disabilities visit remote places, 
such as museums. A study by Natalie Friedman and Alex Cabral (2018) showed that providing telepresence 
robots to children with developmental challenges improved their physical and social self-efficacy as well 
as their overall well-being. Over time, telepresence technology has evolved, incorporating artificial 
intelligence. A notable example is Ava, an AI-powered telepresence robot capable of autonomous 
navigation. Users simply set a destination on their smartphone, and Ava navigates the environment while 
avoiding obstacles. While this automation increases efficiency and safety, it also eliminates the user’s 

active role in controlling the robot, potentially reducing the sense of embodiment and presence that 
comes from physically steering the robot themselves.

5.4.3 Collaborative Tools   
 
Numerous collaborative tools have become integral to our personal and professional routines, such as 
shared calendars, word processing, and project management tools. Some are widely used, like Outlook 
and Google Docs, while others are more specialized for office environments. For instance, Slack is popular 
among software development teams (Lin et al., 2016). Slack is highly flexible, supporting both work-
related and social communication, as well as team management, code sharing, and software 
deployments. It also serves as a knowledge-sharing platform, where team members can stay informed 
about new tools, updates, and other useful information. Developers often keep Slack open while coding 
to exchange quick messages and check availability for impromptu conversations. 
Shared calendars, such as those offered by Google and Outlook, have become essential for managing and 
organizing meetings and events. Many individuals now use multiple color-coded calendars for different 
people or groups, and meeting invitations are commonly sent directly to online calendars. This feature 
has extended to families as well, who use shared calendars to coordinate their children's school events, 
sports schedules, birthdays, and playdates, making it easier to manage and track collective plans. 
Miro gained significant popularity during the pandemic as a tool for online learning. It offers a digital 
canvas with various shared creative tools, including diagramming, mind mapping, and videoconferencing. 
Both tutors and students found it convenient and enjoyable to use, with tools readily available for 
creating, commenting on, and sharing documents in real-time. For example, Nic Marquardt from 
University College London uses Miro in his graduate interaction design course, where students upload 
posters of their design projects to the platform. These posters are displayed side by side for the entire 
class to view, and students can zoom in and out to explore each project (see Figures 5.11a and 5.11b). 
 
During live sessions, students can navigate the Miro canvas, represented by colored and named cursors, 
and provide feedback using digital sticky notes. Staff and teaching assistants offer their feedback using a 
different color. Students are given guidelines prior to the session on how to provide constructive feedback 
to one another.
