3.4 Interface Metaphors   
 
Metaphors play a crucial role in conceptual models by offering a structure that resembles familiar entities, 
while also introducing their own behaviours and properties. More specifically, an interface metaphor is 
one that becomes part of the user interface itself, such as the desktop metaphor. Another notable 
example is the "search engine," a term coined in the early 1990s to describe a software tool that indexes 
and retrieves files from the Internet using algorithms to match user-selected terms. This metaphor draws 
parallels between a mechanical engine, which consists of various working parts, and the everyday action 
of searching for something in different places.

However, the functions of a search engine extend beyond those of a mechanical engine or the way a 
person might search for books in a library. A search engine also organizes and prioritizes the results of a 
search, which is a different process from how mechanical engines or human search methods operate. The 
similarities suggested by the term "search engine" are broad and intended to capture the essence of the 
process of finding relevant information, allowing users to connect these familiar concepts to the less 
familiar features and functions offered by the search engine. 
 
Interface metaphors are designed to draw from familiar entities, allowing users to easily understand the 
underlying conceptual model and navigate the interface. However, these metaphors can sometimes defy 
users' expectations about how things should be, as seen with the recycle bin (or trash can) on the desktop. 
Logically and culturally, it might seem like it should be placed under the desk, but this would have hidden 
it from view due to the desktop surface. To address this, it was placed on the desktop, and although some 
users found this placement odd, most accepted it once they understood the reasoning behind it. 
A common interface metaphor in user experience design is the card. Social media platforms like Twitter 
and Pinterest often display content in digital cards, which are based on the familiar form of physical cards. 
These cards, whether playing cards, business cards, birthday cards, or credit cards, have a long history 
and strong associations, making them an intuitive way to organize content in a compact, "card-sized" 
format. Cards can easily be flicked through, sorted, and grouped, and they break content into meaningful 
chunks—much like paragraphs organize related sentences. Cards have become the standard interface for 
many self-ordering kiosks, as they provide a simple, structured approach to presenting information. 

Metaphors often become deeply integrated into everyday language, influencing how people discuss 
various aspects of technology. For example, terms like "screen time" are commonly used by parents in 
the same way they talk about general time management, and the verb "Googling" has become a standard 
way to refer to searching for information online. The widespread use of these metaphors reflects how 
they have become ingrained in daily conversation. It is often challenging to discuss technology without 
relying on metaphors, as they are so embedded in how we describe our interactions with tools like Twitter 
or Instagram. These platforms are often described through metaphorical terms, such as "posting," 
"following," or "liking," making it difficult to explain them without such language.
