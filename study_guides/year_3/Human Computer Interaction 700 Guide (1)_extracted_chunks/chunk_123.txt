3.3 Conceptual Models  
 
A model is a simplified representation of a system or process that helps explain how it functions. In this 
section, we focus on a specific type of model used in interaction design to clarify the problem and design 
space: the conceptual model. Later, we explore other models that have been developed to explain 
phenomena in human-computer interaction. 
Jeff Johnson and Austin Henderson (2002) first defined a conceptual model as "a high-level description of 
how a system is organized and operates" (p. 26). A key advantage of conceptualizing a design at this level 
is that it allows designers to refine their thinking before focusing on specific elements of the design (p. 
28). In their subsequent handbook on conceptual models, they expanded on this methodology, outlining 
the steps for creating a conceptual model and how to use it throughout the design process (Johnson and 
Henderson, 2012). This involves identifying what users can do with a product and the concepts required 
to understand how to interact with it. 
Essentially, a conceptual model provides a framework of general concepts and their interconnections, 
guiding the design process. The core components of a conceptual model include: 
• Metaphors and analogies that help users understand the product's purpose and how to use it for 
tasks (e.g., browsing, bookmarking). 
• Concepts exposed through the product, such as task-domain objects users create or manipulate, 
their attributes, and the actions that can be performed on them (e.g., saving, revisiting, 
organizing). 
• Relationships between these concepts (e.g., a folder containing files). 
• Mappings between concepts and user experience, showing how the product supports or invokes 
particular user experiences (e.g., revisiting a page via a list of visited or saved websites). 
How these metaphors, concepts, and relationships are organized directly impacts the user experience. By 
discussing these elements, the design team can evaluate different methods of interaction and how they 
support key concepts like saving, revisiting, categorizing, and reorganizing web pages. They can also 
explore whether a new overarching metaphor that combines browsing, searching, and revisiting might be 
more effective. This process can lead the team to identify relationships between the concepts, such as 
containership, and work out the most intuitive way to organize and revisit saved pages.
