2.2.6 A Simple Lifecycle Model for Interaction Design 
 
Understanding the activities involved in interaction design is crucial, but it's equally important to grasp 
how these activities are interconnected. The term "lifecycle model" (or process model) refers to a 
framework that outlines a set of activities and their relationships. These models can vary in complexity 
and are often non-prescriptive. For smaller projects with only a few experienced developers, a 
straightforward process may be sufficient. However, for larger systems with many developers, a simple 
approach is inadequate to provide the necessary structure and discipline for developing a usable product. 

Various lifecycle models have been proposed in fields related to interaction design. For instance, in 
software engineering, popular models include the waterfall, spiral, and V models (for more details, see 
Pressman and Maxim [2019]). While HCI has been less focused on lifecycle models, two well-known 
examples are the Star model (Hartson and Hix, 1989) and the international standard ISO 9241-210. Instead 
of delving into the specifics of these models, we focus on a simpler lifecycle model depicted in Figure 2.4. 
This model illustrates how the four activities of interaction design are interconnected and incorporates 
the principles of people-centered design discussed earlier. 
 
Many projects begin by identifying requirements, which then lead to the creation of alternative designs. 
Prototypes of these designs are developed and evaluated. Based on feedback from evaluations or during 
the prototyping phase, the team may refine the requirements or redesign the product. This iterative cycle 
of refinement may happen with one or more alternative designs running in parallel. The underlying idea 
in this process is that the final product evolves over time, starting from an initial concept to the finished 
product or gradually progressing from basic functionality to a more advanced one. The exact progression 
varies from project to project, but development typically concludes with an evaluation activity to ensure 
the final product meets the expected user experience and usability standards. This evolutionary process 
is reflected on the right side of the double diamond model, though it’s important to note that discovering 
requirements may be revisited during this stage. 

In recent years, numerous lifecycle models have emerged, each encompassing these activities but with 
different focuses on specific activities, relationships, and outcomes. For example, design sprints (Box 2.3) 
prioritize rapid investigation of problems, development of solutions, and testing. While this approach 
doesn’t always result in a fully developed product, it ensures that the solution is acceptable to customers. 
The in-the-wild approach (Box 2.4) focuses on developing novel technologies that may not be tailored to 
specific needs but aim to enhance people, places, and environments. Additional models are discussed in 
Chapter 13.
