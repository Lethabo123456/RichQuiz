Design patterns are a distilled representation of common practices, but one of the challenges with 
common practices is that they are not always effective. Design approaches that embody poor practices 
are known as anti-patterns. A classic example of an anti-pattern is "click here," also referred to as mystery 
navigation. This is problematic because it fails to indicate to the user what action will result from clicking 
the link, leading to poor interaction design. 

Despite significant improvements in interaction design and user experience since the first edition of this 
book in 2002, anti-patterns persist. This is partly because technology evolves rapidly, and design solutions 
that work well on one platform may not be as effective on others. Furthermore, as patterns are used more 
widely, a better understanding of their advantages and drawbacks emerges, and sometimes patterns are 
applied in unintended ways. A prime example is the hamburger menu, originally designed for contextual 
menus but repurposed to save screen space. 

Another important concept in design patterns is dark patterns. While not inherently poor design, dark 
patterns are intentionally crafted to deceive users, prioritizing organizational benefits over user value. 
Some dark patterns are accidental, and once identified, they can be quickly corrected. However, when a 
UX designer intentionally leverages knowledge of human behavior to implement misleading functionality 
that harms the user experience, that is considered a dark pattern. Research by Linda Di Geronimo et al. 
(2020), which analyzed 240 mobile apps with 589 users, found that popular apps often employ at least 
seven types of deceptive interfaces. Examples include preselected options, small close buttons on ads, or 

double negatives in selection text, all of which manipulate the user into making choices that may not align 
with their best interests.
