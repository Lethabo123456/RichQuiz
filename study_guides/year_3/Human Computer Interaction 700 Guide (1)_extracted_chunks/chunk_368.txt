9.2.3 Documentation 
 
Traditionally, UX designers rely on documentation to capture and communicate designs. This includes 
user research results, personas, interface sketches, and wireframes. However, agile development 
emphasizes minimal documentation to prioritize design work, delivering value through a functional 
product. While documentation serves critical purposes—such as legal compliance, maintenance, and 
recording complex design decisions—it should not replace communication and collaboration. Agile UX 
values "minimal" documentation, which does not imply "no documentation," but rather focuses on what 
is necessary. 

Guidelines for determining the appropriate level of documentation often involve asking: 
• How much time is spent on documentation versus design? Can documentation time be reduced? 
• Who uses the documentation? 
• What is the minimum content needed to serve its purpose? Aim for "just barely good enough" 
documentation—adequate but not excessive. 
• How efficient is the sign-off process? Could delays due to approvals be reduced? 
• Are there instances of document duplication? 
• If documentation is primarily for communication or development, how polished does it need to 
be? Could alternative communication methods be more effective? 

The Disciplined Agile approach (PMI, 2022) introduces the CRUFT formula to evaluate documentation 
effectiveness:
 
• Correctness: Percentage of accurate content. 
• Readability: Likelihood the document will be read. 
• Understanding: Likelihood the content will be understood. 
• Follow-through: Likelihood the advice will be acted upon. 
• Trust: Likelihood the advice will be trusted.

Since four of these factors depend on the document's audience, fostering interactions with the end users 
of the document can help refine its value and determine its necessary length. This approach ensures 
documentation is purposeful, efficient, and supportive of the overall agile process.
