2.3.4 How to Choose Among Alternative Designs
  
Choosing between design alternatives often involves making key decisions that affect the final product. 
These decisions might include aspects such as whether to use a physical keyboard or a touchscreen, or 
whether an app should automatically save user data. Such choices are influenced by user research, the 
technical feasibility of ideas, and regulatory considerations, such as security and privacy requirements. 
Broadly, these decisions can be categorized into two types: those related to visible, measurable features 
and those concerning internal system characteristics that can't be observed without a deeper analysis. 
For example, in a printer, visible features include its size, speed, and the paper sizes it supports, while 
internal factors like material choices (e.g., friction ratings) affect the product’s performance but are not 
directly observable. In interaction design, the focus is typically on the user experience, making externally 
visible features the primary concern. Detailed internal workings are relevant primarily when they impact 
external behaviour or features. 

To decide between alternative designs, it is essential to let stakeholders interact with them and share 
their feedback. These designs should be presented in a way that users can easily understand—avoiding 
technical jargon or overly complex descriptions. Prototypes are often used in this process because they 
allow users to experience the design first hand. Prototypes help clarify misunderstandings and test 
technical feasibility, offering better insights into user experience compared to static descriptions. 
Another approach to comparing designs is through A/B testing, where two variations of a product are 
deployed, and real user data is gathered to inform the final decision. This method is commonly used for 
websites, where alternate designs are tested to determine which performs better. 
Quality is another criterion for choosing between alternatives, but quality is subjective and varies from 
one stakeholder to another. For instance, one user might value ease of use in a smartphone, while another 
might prioritize advanced sound settings. To navigate these differences, it’s crucial to understand the 
varying definitions of quality held by different groups of stakeholders. 
In many projects, different stakeholder groups define quality in their own ways. For instance, the term 
“realistic” in a video game might mean different things to developers, teenage players, and parents. 
Capturing these varying views ensures that expectations are clearly defined and provides a benchmark 
for evaluating the product.
