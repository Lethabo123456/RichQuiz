6.5 Persuasive Technologies and Behavioral Change  
 
Various techniques have been employed in interface design to capture users' attention and influence their 
behaviour, often through persuasive design methods. These techniques include pop-up ads, reminders, 
recommendations, and personalized messages aimed at changing what users do or think. For example, 
Amazon's one-click purchase mechanism simplifies buying items, while recommender systems suggest 
products, books, hotels, and restaurants based on users' previous choices and preferences. 
Persuasive design can also be used to influence behaviour in areas like safety, healthcare, fitness, 
sustainability, and education. Early examples of such technology include Nintendo’s Pokémon Pikachu 
device, which motivated children to be more active. This device required users to walk, run, or jump daily 
to keep their virtual pet alive, rewarding them with "watts" for each step. The emotional attachment 
children formed with their virtual pet, combined with rewards and consequences (such as the pet sulking 
when ignored), proved to be an effective motivator for encouraging physical activity.

Various technologies, including fitness trackers (e.g., Fitbit) and smart scales, have been developed to 
help individuals monitor their behaviors, particularly focusing on physical activity and weight 
management. These devices provide real-time feedback through graphs and dashboards, allowing users 
to track progress over days, weeks, and longer periods. They often compare users' results to their peers 
through online leaderboards, encouraging competition and motivation. Additionally, techniques such as 
goal setting, reminders, and rewards are used to promote good habits, like exercising more or moving 
when sedentary. 

In the context of climate change, researchers in Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) have focused on 
developing energy-sensing devices to provide real-time feedback and encourage people to reduce their 
energy consumption. This initiative is part of the larger sustainable HCI movement, aiming to influence 
behaviors related to environmental concerns, such as reducing carbon footprints on an individual, 
community, or organizational level. 
Studies have shown that providing households with feedback on their energy consumption can lead to 
significant reductions, especially when feedback is frequent (daily or continuous). The type of feedback 
representation is critical: overly explicit images can be perceived as invasive, while more subtle and 
engaging visuals (e.g., emoticons or simple infographics) may encourage reflection without causing 
resistance. Social norms and peer pressure also play a role in motivating behavior change. For example, a 
study by Wesley Schultz et al. found that showing households their energy usage compared to the 
neighborhood average led to reductions in consumption, with the addition of emoticons (e.g., smileys 
and sad faces) reinforcing positive or negative behaviors. 

In the Tidy Street project in Brighton, UK, a public display on the street showed daily updates of electricity 
usage, compared to the city's average, using chalk markings on the road. This visual representation 
sparked conversations among neighbors and passersby about energy consumption and resulted in a 15% 
reduction in electricity usage, showcasing the power of public feedback and community engagement.
