6.2 Emotions and Behaviour  
 
Think about the range of emotions you experience during a common activity like shopping online for a 
product, such as a laptop, sofa, or vacation. It often starts with the realization of needing or wanting the 
item, followed by the anticipation and excitement of finding the perfect one. As you begin your search, 
there’s the joy—or sometimes frustration—of discovering what’s available, navigating through countless 
options, and comparing prices, reviews, and recommendations across websites and social media. 
Matching your preferences and needs with what’s available can be thrilling, but that thrill might quickly 
fade if the cost is higher than expected. Disappointment sets in if your top choice is unaffordable, and 
revising your decision may lead to irritation if no alternative matches the initial option. The process of 
revisiting sites and narrowing down options can become increasingly frustrating. 
When you finally settle on a choice, a sense of relief washes over you. But then comes the sometimes 
tedious process of selecting options like color, size, and warranty, followed by the challenge of filling out 
payment forms—where the fear of making an error looms large. After completing the order, there’s often 
a moment of satisfaction, punctuated by a sigh of relief. Yet, doubt may creep in: “Was there a better 
option I overlooked?” 
This rollercoaster of emotions is a familiar experience for many, especially when shopping for big-ticket 
items. The overwhelming number of options and the pressure to make the right choice heighten the 
emotional stakes, turning what seems like a straightforward task into an emotional journey. 

Understanding these distinctions can help design experiences that account for the fluid and complex 
nature of human emotional states.
 
To understand the relationship between emotions and behaviour, it’s important to explore how people 
express themselves and interpret others' expressions. This involves recognizing connections between 
facial expressions, body language, gestures, and tone of voice. For example, happiness is often 
accompanied by smiling, laughter, and a relaxed posture, while anger might manifest as shouting, 
gesticulating, tensed hands, and a contorted face. Emotional expressions often elicit responses in 
others—when someone smiles, it can make others feel good and smile in return. 

Emotional skills, such as expressing and recognizing emotions, are fundamental to human 
communication. Most people are adept at identifying emotions like anger, happiness, sadness, or 
boredom by observing facial expressions, speech patterns, and body signals. They also understand the 
appropriateness of emotions in context, such as empathizing with someone who has failed an exam rather 
than expressing happiness in their presence. 

There is ongoing debate about whether emotions directly cause behaviors. For instance, does anger 
enhance concentration? Does happiness increase risk-taking, such as overspending? Or are emotions 
independent of behaviour? Researchers like Roy Baumeister et al. (2007) propose that the connection 
between emotions and behavior is complex and context-dependent. Similarly, Mayer Tamir and Yochanan 
Bigman (2017) found that expectations influence how emotions shape behavior. In their experiments, 
participants performed more creatively when their emotional state, such as excitement or calmness, was 
framed as conducive to success.
