2.7 Policies, Procedures, and Regulations 
Consider the scenario in which you are the network administrator for a substantial enterprise 
network, overseeing a team of eight technicians responsible for routine installations, upgrades, 
and troubleshooting. It is imperative that you and your technicians reach a consensus on policies 
regarding the addition of new users; failure to do so may result in certain users having insufficient 
access restrictions or inconsistencies in the standard naming convention for logon IDs.  
 
The former issue could lead to security vulnerabilities, while the latter may complicate future 
user management. Adherence to established rules aids in mitigating chaos, confusion, and 
potential downtime for both you and the users. Previous chapters of this book have elaborated 
on the policies, procedures, and regulations conducive to effective network management. 
These are summarized as follows: 
● Media installation and management—This includes designing the physical layout of a cable or 
wireless infrastructure, adhering to best practices for cable management, evaluating the 
performance of cable or wireless infrastructure, and meticulously documenting cable layouts. 
● Network addressing policies—This encompasses selecting and implementing an addressing 
scheme, ascertaining the utilization and limitations of subnets, integrating the addressing of an 
internal network with that of an external network, and configuring gateways for Network Address 
Translation (NAT). 
● Security-related policies—These involve establishing password protocols, restricting access to 
physical spaces such as data centers, limiting access to shared network resources, imposing 
restrictions on the types of files permissible for storage on networked computers, monitoring for 
malware, and conducting regular security audits. 
● Troubleshooting procedures—This entails adopting a systematic approach for resolving 
network issues and documenting resolutions. 
● Backup and disaster recovery procedures—This includes formulating a method and schedule

for creating backups, regularly assessing the efficacy of backups, appointing a disaster recovery 
team and delineating roles, as well as selecting and testing a disaster recovery strategy. 
Beyond internal policies, a network manager must also account for state and federal regulations 
that might influence her responsibilities. In the United States, the Communications Assistance 
for Law Enforcement Act (CALEA), a federal regulation, mandates that telecommunications 
carriers and equipment manufacturers furnish surveillance capabilities. CALEA was enacted by 
Congress in 1994 in response to FBI concerns that digital communication networks could 
potentially bypass conventional wiretapping techniques. To illustrate, a phone call conducted via 
VoIP over a private WAN is not as readily interceptable as one made through the Public Switched 
Telephone Network (PSTN). Consequently, if employed by an Internet Service Provider (ISP), for 
instance, it is essential that your switches and routers facilitate electronic surveillance and that 
your team is prepared to grant access to such devices to authorities upon the presentation of a 
warrant.
