4.1.4 Redundancy and High Availability (HA) Concepts 

Organizations must identify and manage the risks that could interrupt their operations — a 
process known as risk management. A core part of risk management in IT involves ensuring that 
services remain available even during failures. This is where redundancy and high availability 
(HA) come into play. These strategies are used to minimize downtime and maintain continuity in 
the event of hardware failures, natural disasters, or service disruptions. 
One of the most important HA strategies is the use of recovery sites, which act as backup 
locations if the primary data center goes oﬄine. Recovery sites diﬀer in terms of cost, setup, 
and how quickly they can take over operations. A cold site is the most basic and least expensive 
option. 

It includes only the physical infrastructure like power, air conditioning, and wiring—no 
servers or networking equipment. Since everything must be brought in and set up after a 
disaster occurs, cold sites take the longest to restore and are the hardest to test. 
A warm site oﬀers a balance between cost and recovery speed. It includes power, networking 
equipment, and communications infrastructure. The main task during recovery is restoring the 
software and data, making it faster than a cold site. Warm sites are commonly used because 
they are easier to manage and test compared to cold sites. 

At the high end of the spectrum is the hot site, which is fully equipped with servers, networking 
equipment, power systems, and sometimes even live data replication. A hot site can take over 
operations within minutes, but maintaining this level of readiness is expensive. It also requires 
strict security controls and matching infrastructure to that of the primary site. 
An increasingly popular option is the cloud-based recovery site. This virtual environment 
mimics the organization’s IT infrastructure using cloud resources. It’s especially useful for 
smaller organizations that lack the capital or expertise to build physical recovery sites. With 
cloud disaster recovery, systems and data can be restored quickly using cloud-hosted virtual 
machines and storage.

Another key HA concept is the conﬁguration of systems in either active/active or active/passive 
modes. In an active/active setup, all systems are operational and share the workload. This 
improves performance and availability but requires more careful coordination. In contrast, 
active/passive conﬁgurations involve a primary system actively handling operations while a 
secondary system remains on standby, ready to take over if the primary fails. This approach is 
simpler and oﬀers strong fault tolerance. 

Internet connectivity is another critical area where redundancy plays a role. A single internet 
path to one ISP is a point of failure. To avoid this, organizations can implement path 
redundancy, where two routers connect to the same ISP through diﬀerent physical routes. 
However, if the ISP itself fails, this isn't enough. In that case, ISP redundancy—using multiple 
internet service providers—is a more robust solution. The highest level of redundancy combines 
both methods: two routers connected to two diﬀerent ISPs, protecting against both internal and 
external failures. 

Multiple Internet Service Providers (ISPs)/Diverse Paths 
 
Redundancy can also enhance the reliability of your Internet connection. There are two main 
ways to implement it. Path redundancy involves conﬁguring multiple routes to the same Internet 
Service Provider (ISP), typically using two diﬀerent routers. This setup ensures continued 
connectivity if one router or path fails. However, if the ISP itself experiences a failure, having 
multiple paths won’t help. In that case, you can achieve ISP redundancy by connecting to two 
diﬀerent ISPs, even if through a single router. This protects your network from both hardware 
and provider-side issues.
