2.4.2 Congestion Control 
Flow control is responsible for regulating the number of frames sent between a single sender and 
receiver, whereas congestion control oversees the overall traffic volume across the network. 
Consider a switch or router's viewpoint when assessing network performance: Are there too 
many devices sending messages simultaneously? Is one host monopolizing network resources by 
transmitting excessive data? Such issues can overwhelm the network. When congestion occurs, 
messages may become corrupted or dropped, leading devices to resend frames to compensate. 
This reaction generates additional traffic, worsening the congestion. Congestion control 
strategies aim to prevent congestion (known as open-loop congestion control) and alleviate it 
afterward (known as closed-loop congestion control). Open-loop tactics include these policies:
 
• Retransmission policy—Retransmission timers help mitigate congestion intensified by devices 
attempting to resend lost packets either too promptly or too frequently. 
• Window policy—Senders may be required to use the selective repeat sliding window method 
to minimize the frames needing retransmission when errors occur. 
• Acknowledgment policy—Receivers might send a single ACK message for multiple frames, 
reducing acknowledgment traffic. 
• Discarding policy—Less critical frames are discarded to ensure vital traffic persists through 
congestion. 
• Admission policy—Routers and switches can temporarily deny new traffic to prevent or ease 
congestion rather than allowing it onto the network. The closed-loop approach involves: 
• Implicit signaling—A sender identifies congestion after multiple missed acknowledgments. 
• Explicit signaling—A congested device modifies data packets to alert the sender (backward 
signaling) or receiver (forward signaling) of congestion. 
• Choke packet—A congested router generates a choke packet to inform the traffic source of 
congestion, prompting it to slow its transmission rate. 
• Backpressure—A node downstream ceases to accept traffic, transferring congestion pressure 
upstream. This method is applicable to specific congestion scenarios. While congestion control 
governs traffic entry onto the network, other techniques allow nuanced traffic management once 
it’s in transit, where QoS plays a role, as discussed next.
